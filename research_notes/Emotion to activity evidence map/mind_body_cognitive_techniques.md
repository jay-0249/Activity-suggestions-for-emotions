# Mind-body and cognitive self-regulation techniques: evidence for effects on specific emotional states

**Verification note (read first).** In this session direct page fetches to PubMed/PMC/publisher sites were blocked by the network allowlist, so no full text could be opened. Every figure below was checked against abstract text or repository records returned by web search. Three tags are used:

- **[A]** = number matches abstract/repository text returned by search (abstract-level verified, full text not opened).
- **[S]** = number comes only from a secondary summary (press release, later review, thesis abstract); treat as provisional.
- **[U]** = unverified in this session (recalled from prior knowledge or not found); do not ship as a hard number without checking.

Effect sizes are standardized mean differences (g or d) unless stated. Sign conventions differ between papers; direction is stated in words.

## Q1. For each technique, which emotions/states does it measurably improve, with what effect size and study type?

### Takeaway
The best-supported links are: arousal-lowering practices (slow breathing, relaxation, mindfulness) for anger, anxiety and stress (g about 0.3 to 0.6); behavioural activation for low mood (SMD about 0.74 in depressed samples, 0.52 for well-being); self-compassion for stress, depression and rumination (g about 0.6 to 0.7, larger for rumination but from few trials); and cognitive change strategies (reappraisal, perspective-taking) for momentary negative emotion (d+ 0.36 to 0.45). Gratitude, brief mindfulness, micro-breaks and positive psychology exercises have small effects (g about 0.2 to 0.4) that shrink further against active controls or after publication-bias correction. Grounding (5-4-3-2-1), counting to ten, hydration and box breathing in isolation have little or no direct trial evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Breathing: slow-paced / diaphragmatic, cyclic sighing, box breathing**
- Breathwork meta-analysis of RCTs: lower self-reported stress vs controls, g = -0.35 (95% CI -0.55 to -0.14), k = 12, N = 785; anxiety (k = 20) and depressive symptoms (k = 18) showed similar significant effects, with g = -0.32 reported for one of them (the abstract snippet did not show which). Most studies had moderate risk of bias; the authors call the evidence preliminary. [A] — [Fincham et al. 2023, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/) (doi:10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)
- Remote RCT, n = 108, 5 min/day for 1 month, four arms (cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation with retention, mindfulness meditation). All arms improved mood; breathwork arms gained more daily positive affect than mindfulness (about 1.91 vs 1.22 PANAS points per day [S]); cyclic sighing gave the largest positive-affect gain and a larger drop in resting respiratory rate. No between-group differences in heart rate or HRV. No no-treatment control arm. — [Balban et al. 2023, Cell Reports Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947); summary at [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/insights/2023/02/cyclic-sighing-can-help-breathe-away-anxiety.html)
- Box breathing has no independent meta-analytic evidence found; its only direct trial evidence here is as one arm of Balban 2023, where it was not singled out as superior to mindfulness. — [Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)
- Systematic review of slow breathing (<10 breaths/min) in healthy adults, 15 studies from 2,461 abstracts: higher HRV and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, more EEG alpha and less theta; psychological outcomes included more comfort, relaxation and vigor and less anxiety, depression, anger and confusion. Qualitative synthesis only, no pooled effect size. [A] — [Zaccaro et al. 2018, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full) (doi:10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353)
- Meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing, 223 studies: vagally mediated HRV increased during a session, immediately after a single session, and after multi-session interventions; authors expect few adverse effects. Pooled effect sizes were not visible in the abstract. [A] — [Laborde et al. 2022, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/) (doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)
- In a 20-min single-session RCT (n = 60 students), deep breathing raised self-reported relaxation vs control but produced a brief initial spike in physiological arousal before returning to baseline, unlike PMR and guided imagery. [A] — [Toussaint et al. 2021, Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/177973/) (doi:10.1155/2021/5924040)

**Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), guided imagery, relaxation training**
- Relaxation training for anxiety, 27 studies (1997 to 2007): Cohen's d = 0.57 within-group and 0.51 between-group (medium to large). Subgroup tables list within-group effects of about 0.82 for progressive relaxation, 0.73 applied relaxation, 0.67 autogenic training, 0.66 meditation. The between-group CI printed in the abstract (.46 to .634) looks internally inconsistent. [A] — [Manzoni et al. 2008, BMC Psychiatry](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2427027/) (doi:10.1186/1471-244X-8-41)
- Single 20-min audio-guided session: PMR, deep breathing and guided imagery each significantly increased self-reported relaxation vs control; PMR and guided imagery produced an immediate linear decline in electrodermal activity and heart rate. Effect sizes not visible. [A] — [Toussaint et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/177973/)
- PMR, deep breathing and mindfulness are named among effective arousal-decreasing activities for anger (see anger section). — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024 summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm)

**Mindfulness meditation (programs and brief training), body scan**
- 47 trials, 3,515 participants: meditation programs produce small to moderate reductions in negative dimensions of psychological stress; moderate-strength evidence for anxiety, depression and pain, low evidence for stress/distress and quality of life; no evidence of superiority over active treatments (exercise, drugs, other behavioural therapies). Exact SMDs (commonly quoted as about 0.38 for anxiety and 0.30 for depression at 8 weeks) could not be confirmed here. [A for conclusions, U for the two SMDs] — [Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/)
- MBSR in healthy adults, 29 studies, n = 2,668: moderately effective for stress, depression, anxiety and distress and for quality of life. Exact Hedges g values not visible. [A] — [Khoury et al. 2015, Journal of Psychosomatic Research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25818837/)
- Brief mindfulness training (65 RCTs, 5,489 mostly meditation-naive participants, mean age 24.6): small reduction in negative affectivity, g = 0.21; community samples g = 0.41 vs students g = 0.14; after publication-bias correction the overall effect fell to g = 0.04. [A] — [Schumer, Lindsay & Creswell 2018, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6441958)
- In Balban 2023, 5 min/day of mindfulness meditation improved mood and anxiety over a month but less than breathwork on positive affect. — [Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)

**Loving-kindness / compassion meditation**
- Kindness-based meditation, 22 RCTs: reduced self-reported depression, g = -0.61 (95% CI -1.08 to -0.14); increased mindfulness g = 0.63. Positive-emotion estimate rested on only two studies; authors call for larger trials. [A] — [Galante et al. 2014, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24979314/) (doi:10.1037/a0037249)
- LKM and positive emotions, 24 studies, N = 1,759: medium effects on daily positive emotions in wait-list RCTs; small to large effects of ongoing practice on immediate positive emotions; loving-kindness-focused interventions medium, compassion-focused small; length of intervention and time spent meditating did not moderate. Exact g values not visible. [A] — [Zeng et al. 2015, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693/full) (doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693)

**Self-compassion exercises**
- 27 RCTs: self-compassion g = 0.75, stress g = 0.67, depression g = 0.66, anxiety g = 0.57, self-criticism g = 0.56, rumination g = 1.37 (large, few studies). Stronger for group than individual delivery; possible publication bias; interventions too heterogeneous to compare formats. [A] — [Ferrari et al. 2019, Mindfulness](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12671-019-01134-6) (doi:10.1007/s12671-019-01134-6)

**Gratitude (list, letter)**
- Three experiments (weekly lists, daily lists for 13 days, and adults with neuromuscular disease): gratitude groups showed heightened well-being on several but not all outcomes; the effect on positive affect was the most robust finding. [A] — [Emmons & McCullough 2003, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12585811/)
- 27 studies, N = 3,675: small effect on depression and anxiety symptoms, g = -0.29 at post-test and -0.23 at follow-up; larger against waitlist than active controls. Authors call effects modest and recommend better-supported treatments for depression and anxiety. [A] — [Cregg & Cheavens 2021, Journal of Happiness Studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6) (doi:10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6; online 2020, vol. 22, 2021)
- Gratitude interventions beat measurement-only controls on psychological well-being, d = 0.31 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.58, k = 5), but not on gratitude itself, d = 0.20 (ns), and were no better than psychologically active comparisons, d = -0.03 (k = 9). [A, via catalogue record] — [Davis et al. 2016, Journal of Counseling Psychology](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1760876509) (doi:10.1037/cou0000107)

**Savoring and positive psychology interventions (PPIs) in general**
- PPIs, 39 RCTs: SMD 0.34 subjective well-being, 0.20 psychological well-being, 0.23 depression; small effects persisting at 3 to 6 months for well-being. [A] — [Bolier et al. 2013, BMC Public Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23390882/) (doi:10.1186/1471-2458-13-119)
- Re-analysis correcting small-sample bias: well-being effects small but significant; depression effects variable, outlier-dependent and generally not significant. [A] — [White, Uttl & Holder 2019, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6541265/)
- PPIs, 347 studies, >72,000 participants: well-being g = 0.39, depression g = -0.39 [S, from a secondary review]; anxiety and stress figures not confirmed. — [Carr et al. 2021, Journal of Positive Psychology](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760.2020.1818807) (doi:10.1080/17439760.2020.1818807)
- Savoring specifically: a recent meta-analysis of 20 RCTs (N = 4,805) reports g = 0.51 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.77) overall and g = 0.50 for positive emotional states. Seen only through an aggregator listing; citation details not confirmed. [S] — [listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2026/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/aphw-70134/)
- PPIs raise positive affect, d = 0.51 vs controls, with small effects on negative affect and depressive symptoms. [S] — [Carrillo et al. 2019, PLoS ONE](https://rua.ua.es/dspace/bitstream/10045/96967/1/2019_Carrillo_etal_PLoS-ONE.pdf)

**Cognitive reappraisal, distraction, suppression**
- Meta-analysis of experimentally instructed emotion regulation strategies: cognitive change d+ = 0.36 overall; reappraising the stimulus 0.36, perspective-taking 0.45, reappraising one's emotional response 0.23; distraction d+ = 0.27; concentrating on feelings d+ = -0.26 (makes things worse); expressive suppression 0.32, suppressing the experience -0.04, thought suppression -0.12; attentional deployment overall 0.00; response modulation 0.16. [A] — [Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, Psychological Bulletin](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)

**Self-distancing / third-person self-talk**
- Experiments in which people reflected on anger- or anxiety-provoking experiences using their own name / non-first-person pronouns reported lower negative emotional reactions than first-person reflection. Effect sizes not visible. [S] — [Kross et al. 2014, JPSP, "Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism"](https://psychologyinaction.org/?p=1221) (doi:10.1037/a0035173 [U])
- Third-person self-talk reduced the late positive potential (ERP marker of emotional reactivity) within 1 s of viewing aversive images without increasing cognitive-control markers, and reduced medial prefrontal activity in fMRI; described as a relatively effortless form of self-control. Neural outcomes, not clinical ones. [A] — [Moser et al. 2017, Scientific Reports](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2017/07/talking-to-yourself-in-the-third-person-can-help-you-control-stressful-emotions)

**Affect labeling ("name it to tame it")**
- fMRI, n = 30: labeling the emotion in negative images reduced amygdala and limbic response and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity, with the two inversely correlated; labeling gender/name did not. [A] — [Lieberman et al. 2007, Psychological Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/) (doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)
- Narrative review: affect labeling acts as implicit emotion regulation, with experiential, autonomic, neural and behavioural effects resembling reappraisal. No pooled effect size. [A] — [Torre & Lieberman 2018, Emotion Review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706) (doi:10.1177/1754073917742706)

**Worry postponement / scheduled worry time**
- Meta-analysis of 7 randomized trials, N = 999 (a later version reports 8 samples): postponing worries to a fixed 30-min window vs merely logging them gave small reductions in worry duration (d = 0.313) and worry frequency (d = 0.189). Mostly female non-clinical samples, no follow-up; the earlier effect on somatic complaints did not replicate online. [A] — [Dippel, Brosschot & Verkuil, "Effects of worry postponement on daily worry: a meta-analysis"](https://aminer.org/pub/648e81c1d68f896efa5fa157/effects-of-worry-postponement-on-daily-worry-a-meta-analysis); [Leiden repository copy](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A4105123/download)

**To-do list writing / planning (overwhelm, unfinished tasks)**
- Polysomnography experiment, n = 57 students: 5 min writing a to-do list of upcoming tasks at bedtime led to faster sleep onset than writing a list of completed tasks; more specific lists predicted faster sleep onset. No no-writing control; healthy sleepers. Minutes saved not visible in the abstract (commonly cited as about 9 min [U]). [A] — [Scullin et al. 2018, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/01/180111224021.htm) (doi:10.1037/xge0000374)

**Behavioural activation / pleasant activity scheduling**
- BA vs controls for depression: SMD = -0.74 (95% CI -0.91 to -0.56), k = 25, N = 1,088; vs medication SMD = -0.42 (k = 4). Study quality mostly low, follow-ups short. [A] — [Ekers et al. 2014, PLoS ONE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24936656/) (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0100100)
- BA for well-being, 20 studies, N = 1,353, including non-depressed samples: pooled g = 0.52 at post-test. [S, figure from the author's thesis abstract] — [Mazzucchelli, Kane & Rees 2010, Journal of Positive Psychology](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760903569154) (doi:10.1080/17439760903569154)

**Anger: arousal-decreasing activities, time-out/counting, venting**
- 154 studies, 184 samples, N = 10,189: arousal-decreasing activities (deep breathing, mindfulness, meditation, PMR, relaxation) reduced anger and aggression, g = -0.63 (95% CI -0.82 to -0.43), robust across gender, age, culture, lab/field and delivery mode. Arousal-increasing activities (venting, hitting a bag, jogging) were ineffective overall, g = -0.02 (CI spans zero); jogging tended to increase anger. [A] — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024, Clinical Psychology Review](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/effective-anger-reduction-techniques-revealed-in-comprehensive-review-of-154-studies/)
- Experiment: angered participants who hit a punching bag while thinking of the provoker (rumination) ended up angriest and most aggressive; distraction was intermediate; doing nothing was more effective than venting. [A] — [Bushman 2002, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~bbushman/PSPB02.pdf) (doi:10.1177/0146167202289002)

**Distraction (games, Tetris)**
- Distraction reduces negative emotion, d+ = 0.27. [A] — [Webb et al. 2012](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)
- Lab experiment, n = 40: 10 min of Tetris 30 min after a distressing film led to fewer than half as many intrusive memories over the following week. [S] — [Holmes et al. 2009, PLoS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/f4e7fa369b04443683f317ab413886cf)
- Proof-of-concept RCT in emergency-department trauma patients: brief Tetris-based intervention gave fewer intrusive memories in the following week than attention control; authors call for a larger trial. [A] — [Iyadurai et al. 2018, Molecular Psychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5822451)

**Humor / laughter**
- Systematic review and meta-analysis of multi-session laughter-inducing therapies: simulated (non-humorous, e.g. laughter yoga) laughter more effective than spontaneous (humorous) laughter; laughter therapies can improve depression. Overall study quality low, substantial risk of bias in all studies. Pooled SMDs not visible. [A] — [van der Wal & Kok 2019, Social Science & Medicine](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/143061/) (doi:10.1016/j.socscimed.2019.02.018)

**Short breaks / digital detox**
- Micro-breaks (10 min or less), 22 samples, N = 2,335: vigor d = 0.36 (k = 9), fatigue d = 0.35 (k = 9), performance d = 0.16 (ns); longer breaks helped performance more; highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 min. [A] — [Albulescu et al. 2022, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/) (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)

**Hot bath / shower**
- Meta-analysis (17 studies, 13 pooled): a warm bath or shower 1 to 2 h before bed, for as little as 10 min, shortened sleep onset latency and improved sleep quality. Pooled numbers not visible. [A/S] — [Haghayegh et al. 2019, Sleep Medicine Reviews](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1301348/before-bedtime-passive-body-heating-by-warm-shower) (doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2019.04.008)
- Pilot RCT, 36 depressed outpatients (HAM-D >= 18): 40 C baths twice weekly; HAM-D difference of 3.14 points vs sham at 2 weeks (p = .037). Clinical sample, small, long hot baths. [A] — [Naumann et al. 2017, BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine](https://doaj.org/article/ed05410fa6ad45bdba075d7a60f6e213)

**Aromatherapy**
- Lavender, 100+ RCTs: inhalation reduced anxiety, Hedges g = -0.73 (95% CI -1.00 to -0.46), 1,682 participants; high heterogeneity and high risk of bias in most trials, so the authors call inhalation evidence only "an indication of an effect". [A] — [Donelli et al. 2019, Phytomedicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655395/) (doi:10.1016/j.phymed.2019.153099)

**Tea / hydration**
- Systematic review, 9 RCTs: 200 to 400 mg/day of L-theanine supplement may reduce stress and anxiety in people under stressful conditions. This is supplement-dose evidence (a cup of tea contains far less), not evidence for drinking tea. [A] — [Williams et al. 2020, Plant Foods for Human Nutrition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31758301/) (doi:10.1007/s11130-019-00771-5)

### Inferences
- For an app, the defensible "strong" tier is: arousal-decreasing practice for anger; relaxation/PMR and slow breathing for anxiety and stress; behavioural activation for low mood; self-compassion for stress, self-criticism and rumination. A "moderate" tier: reappraisal/perspective-taking, distraction, brief mindfulness, LKM for positive affect, micro-breaks for fatigue, worry postponement. "Preliminary": cyclic sighing specifically, third-person self-talk, affect labeling, to-do list writing, laughter, aromatherapy, warm bath, Tetris.
- Most effect sizes come from multi-week programs or clinical/student samples; applying them to a single 2 to 30 min self-guided session in general adults is an extrapolation and the true momentary effect is probably smaller.
- Effects against active controls are consistently smaller than against waitlist (gratitude, mindfulness, PPIs), so much of the benefit may be non-specific (doing something structured and pleasant).

### Gaps
- Grounding 5-4-3-2-1: no peer-reviewed trial or meta-analysis was found or searched successfully; it should be treated as clinical-practice lore, justified only indirectly by distraction (d+ = 0.27) and sensory-focused mindfulness evidence.
- Counting to ten / time-out for anger: no standalone trial verified. Press coverage of Kjærvik & Bushman 2024 lists "taking a timeout" among arousal-decreasing activities [U]; the pooled g = -0.63 is for the whole category.
- Body scan: no body-scan-specific meta-analysis was retrieved; evidence is indirect via MBSR/brief mindfulness.
- Box breathing: no standalone efficacy data beyond one trial arm.
- Problem-solving for overwhelm: problem-solving therapy meta-analyses exist for depression but were not retrieved; only Scullin 2018 (sleep onset) was verified for list writing.
- Hydration and mood, digital detox (Radtke et al. 2022 systematic review): not retrieved. No claim should be made.
- Loneliness and boredom as outcomes: none of the retrieved sources measured them directly.
- Exact numbers not confirmed: Goyal 2014 SMDs, Khoury 2015 g values, Zeng 2015 g values, Laborde 2022 pooled effects, van der Wal & Kok SMDs, Haghayegh pooled values, Scullin minutes.

## Q2. Minimum effective dose; are single brief sessions effective?

### Takeaway
Single sessions of 5 to 20 minutes do produce measurable immediate changes for breathing (HRV), relaxation/PMR/imagery (self-reported and physiological relaxation), instructed reappraisal/distraction, third-person self-talk and affect labeling (seconds), LKM (immediate positive emotion) and micro-breaks (10 min or less). Symptom-level change in depression, anxiety or stress has been shown mainly with repeated practice over weeks.

### Cited Findings
- 5 min/day for 28 days of cyclic sighing, box breathing or mindfulness improved daily mood; benefits accrued across days. — [Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)
- vmHRV rises during and immediately after a single slow-breathing session, and after multi-session training. — [Laborde et al. 2022](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/)
- One 20-min audio session of PMR, deep breathing or guided imagery increased relaxation vs control. — [Toussaint et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/177973/)
- Brief mindfulness training (single sessions up to about 2 weeks) has a small effect on negative affect (g = 0.21) that may be near zero after bias correction (g = 0.04). — [Schumer et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6441958)
- LKM: ongoing practice produces immediate positive emotion; total meditation time did not moderate outcomes. — [Zeng et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693/full)
- Emotion regulation strategies in Webb et al. were mostly single-instruction lab manipulations, so d+ values (reappraisal 0.36, distraction 0.27) are effectively single-use effects. — [Webb et al. 2012](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)
- Third-person self-talk changes neural reactivity within 1 second. — [Moser et al. 2017](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2017/07/talking-to-yourself-in-the-third-person-can-help-you-control-stressful-emotions)
- 5 min of to-do list writing changed sleep onset the same night. — [Scullin et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/01/180111224021.htm)
- Micro-breaks of 10 min or less improve vigor and fatigue; performance recovery from demanding work may need longer. — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- 10 min of Tetris after an analogue trauma reduced later intrusions. — [Holmes et al. 2009](https://doaj.org/article/f4e7fa369b04443683f317ab413886cf)
- Gratitude letter protocols typically use 8 to 10 min of writing; gratitude lists were weekly or daily for 2 to 10 weeks. — [Layous & Lyubomirsky overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4380267); [Emmons & McCullough 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12585811/)
- Worry postponement uses one fixed 30-min worry window per day, typically for about a week. — [Dippel et al. meta-analysis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A4105123/download)
- Warm bath/shower: as little as 10 min, 1 to 2 h before bed. — [Haghayegh et al. 2019](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1301348/before-bedtime-passive-body-heating-by-warm-shower)

### Inferences
- An app can honestly say "works within one session" for calming physiology and momentary mood (breathing, PMR, imagery, reappraisal, distraction, labeling, micro-breaks), and "builds with repetition" for gratitude, self-compassion, mindfulness, behavioural activation and worry postponement.
- 5 minutes is a defensible minimum for breathing; 10 to 20 minutes for PMR/imagery/LKM; 5 to 10 minutes for writing exercises.

### Gaps
- No dose-response curves were retrieved for any technique; minimum doses above are "smallest dose tested", not established thresholds.
- Single-session effects of self-compassion and behavioural activation were not retrieved.

## Q3. Verification status of the specifically requested sources

### Takeaway
All requested sources exist and their headline findings were confirmed at abstract level, with corrections: Webb's suppression figure is 0.32 (distraction is 0.27); the laughter meta-analysis is van der Wal & Kok 2019 (not "van der Zwan"); Cregg & Cheavens was published online in 2020; Schumer's g = 0.21 drops to 0.04 after bias correction. Several commonly quoted numbers could not be confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- Balban 2023: confirmed design and direction; PANAS figures from secondary coverage. — [PMC9873947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)
- Fincham 2023: confirmed g = -0.35 stress. — [PMC9828383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/)
- Zaccaro 2018: confirmed, qualitative. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)
- Laborde 2022: confirmed direction; note abstract subtotals (172 + 16 + 49) exceed 223 because studies contribute to more than one time point. — [repository](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/)
- Goyal 2014: conclusions confirmed, SMDs not. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/)
- Khoury 2015: conclusions confirmed, g values not. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25818837/)

## Q4. Contraindications and ways these techniques backfire

### Takeaway
None of these techniques is risk-free: meditation produces transient adverse effects in a meaningful minority, fast/hyperventilation-style breathwork should be kept out of a general app, reappraisal is a poor first choice at high emotional intensity, solitary focus on feelings can amplify them, venting worsens anger, and gratitude has weak effects on depression and may not suit everyone.

### Cited Findings
- Meditation adverse events, 83 studies, 6,703 participants: pooled prevalence 8.3% (95% CI 5 to 12%); 3.7% in experimental studies vs 33.2% in observational ones; anxiety and depression most common; events also occurred in people with no mental health history. [A] — [Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)
- In 8-week mindfulness-based programs (n = 96), 83% reported at least one meditation-related side effect, 58% one with negative valence, 37% one with negative impact on functioning, 6% lasting bad effects over a month. [A] — [Britton et al. 2021, Clinical Psychological Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8636531)
- Deep breathing can transiently increase physiological arousal at onset. — [Toussaint et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/177973/)
- Breathwork evidence is preliminary with moderate risk of bias; the lead author cautioned against extrapolation. Cyclic hyperventilation with breath retention was one tested arm and was not the best performer. — [Fincham et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)
- Emotion-regulation choice: across three experiments people preferred reappraisal for low-intensity negative stimuli and distraction for high-intensity ones, consistent with distraction blocking emotional information before it builds. [A] — [Sheppes, Scheibe, Suri & Gross 2011, Psychological Science](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/emotion-regulation-choice/); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5141964)
- Concentrating on one's feelings worsens emotional outcomes (d+ = -0.26); thought suppression does not help (d+ = -0.12). — [Webb et al. 2012](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)
- Venting while thinking about the provoker increases anger and aggression; arousal-increasing activities do not reduce anger and jogging may increase it. — [Bushman 2002](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~bbushman/PSPB02.pdf); [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm)
- Gratitude interventions have only modest effects on depression/anxiety symptoms and are no better than active controls; authors advise against relying on them as treatment. — [Cregg & Cheavens 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6); [Davis et al. 2016](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1760876509)
- Some studies of PPIs in depressed samples found no benefit over control or placebo. — [Layous & Lyubomirsky overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4380267)
- Lavender/aromatherapy trials are heterogeneous with high risk of bias. — [Donelli et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655395/)

### Inferences
- App guardrails that follow from the evidence: keep meditations short and eyes-open/externally anchored optional; offer an exit and an alternative if a practice increases distress; exclude hyperventilation-style breathwork; route very intense states to breathing, PMR or distraction before any reflective or reappraisal task; never suggest "letting it out" physically for anger; do not offer gratitude or savoring as the sole response to severe low mood, and pair it with behavioural activation.
- Unstructured solitary reflection on a problem resembles "concentration" (d+ = -0.26), so reflective prompts should be structured (distanced, time-limited, action-oriented).

### Gaps
- The claim that gratitude letters reduced well-being in dysphoric participants (Sin, Della Porta & Lyubomirsky 2011) could not be located or verified [U]; do not cite it as fact.
- Sheppes et al. 2014 (JEP: General) was not retrieved. Evidence here is about strategy preference; direct evidence that reappraisal is less effective at high intensity was not verified in this session.
- No peer-reviewed source on medical cautions for breath holding/hyperventilation (pregnancy, epilepsy, cardiovascular disease, panic) was retrieved [U]; such cautions are standard practice guidance, not verified findings here.
- Hot bath safety limits and essential-oil safety were not researched.

## Q5. Which techniques suit high-arousal vs low-arousal states?

### Takeaway
High-arousal negative states (anger, anxiety, acute stress, panic-like overwhelm) respond to arousal-decreasing, low-cognitive-load techniques; low-arousal negative states (sadness, fatigue, boredom) are better matched by activating techniques. The anger evidence for this matching is direct and strong; for the other states it is an inference from separate literatures.

### Cited Findings
- For anger, lowering arousal works (g = -0.63) and raising it does not (g = -0.02). — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm)
- Slow breathing raises vagal HRV within a session; cyclic sighing lowers respiratory rate. — [Laborde et al. 2022](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/); [Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)
- PMR and guided imagery lower electrodermal activity and heart rate immediately; relaxation training reduces anxiety (d about 0.5). — [Toussaint et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/177973/); [Manzoni et al. 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2427027/)
- At high intensity people choose distraction over reappraisal; third-person self-talk regulates without demanding cognitive control. — [Sheppes et al. 2011](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/emotion-regulation-choice/); [Moser et al. 2017](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2017/07/talking-to-yourself-in-the-third-person-can-help-you-control-stressful-emotions)
- For low mood, increasing activity works (BA SMD -0.74; well-being g = 0.52). — [Ekers et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24936656/); [Mazzucchelli et al. 2010](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760903569154)
- For fatigue, short breaks raise vigor (d = 0.36) and reduce fatigue (d = 0.35). — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- For low positive affect, LKM (medium effect on daily positive emotion), gratitude (positive affect most robust outcome) and PPIs (d about 0.5 on positive affect [S]) raise positive emotion. — [Zeng et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693/full); [Emmons & McCullough 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12585811/)
- For worry and rumination (cognitive rather than arousal problems): worry postponement (d = 0.31 duration), self-compassion (rumination g = 1.37), to-do list writing. — [Dippel et al.](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A4105123/download); [Ferrari et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12671-019-01134-6)

### Inferences
- Suggested routing: anger -> slow breathing, PMR, time-out, then distanced self-talk or reappraisal once calmer; never venting. Anxiety/acute stress -> cyclic sighing or slow breathing, PMR, guided imagery, affect labeling; then worry postponement or reappraisal. Overwhelm -> breathing first, then to-do list/planning. Sadness/low mood -> behavioural activation, self-compassion, LKM; avoid long passive body-focused practice as the only option. Fatigue -> micro-break; boredom -> pleasant activity or an engaging game (inferred, no direct boredom data). Loneliness -> LKM is the only technique here with a social-connection rationale (inferred, no direct loneliness data). Positive states -> savoring, gratitude.
- Relaxation practices for someone already low-arousal and sad are not contraindicated by the data, but nothing retrieved shows they lift low mood as well as activation does.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head trial tests matching technique to arousal level outside anger.
- No retrieved evidence addresses boredom or loneliness as outcomes of these techniques.
