## Q1. For each activity, which emotions/states does it measurably improve, with what effect size and study type?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced links are: any exercise → depression/anxiety/distress (medium effects, umbrella-review level, but mostly multi-week programmes and often clinical samples); a single 10–40 min bout of low-to-moderate aerobic activity → more energy/positive activated affect (about half an SD) and a small drop in state anxiety; calm/low-arousal movement (yoga, slow breathing-type activity) → anger and anxiety; nature contact of 10–30 min → stress physiology, positive affect and (at longer doses) rumination. Evidence is thin or absent for boredom, loneliness, stretching, acute dance, sunlight (observational only) and cold exposure (small/uncontrolled).

### Cited Findings

**All physical activity (pooled)**
- Umbrella review of 97 systematic reviews (1,039 trials, 128,119 participants): physical activity vs usual care gave median effect −0.43 (IQR −0.66 to −0.27) for depression, −0.42 (IQR −0.66 to −0.26) for anxiety, and pooled −0.60 (95% CI −0.78 to −0.42) for psychological distress. Verified from abstract. — [Singh et al. 2023, Br J Sports Med, PMID 36796860](https://www.pubmed.gov/36796860/) (doi 10.1136/bjsports-2022-106195)
- Secondary coverage rendering −0.43 as "43% reduction" is a misreading of a standardized effect size; do not repeat it. — [Healio Q&A](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20230803/qa-physical-activity-is-an-effective-intervention-for-mental-health-conditions)

**Brisk walking / running / aerobic exercise**
- Network meta-analysis of 218 studies, 495 arms, 14,170 participants with depression: walking or jogging, yoga and strength training were more effective than other exercise, "particularly when intense"; confidence (CINeMA) was low for walking/jogging and very low for other modalities. Verified from abstract. — [Noetel et al. 2024, BMJ 384:e075847, PMID 38355154](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38355154/?dopt=Abstract) (doi 10.1136/bmj-2023-075847; erratum BMJ 2024;385:q1024)
- Per-modality Hedges g vs active controls in Noetel 2024 — walking/jogging −0.62, yoga −0.55, strength −0.49, mixed aerobic −0.43, tai chi/qigong −0.42, dance −0.96 (dance from few small trials): **[unverified — recalled from abstract, not re-confirmed in this session]**. — [Noetel et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38355154/?dopt=Abstract)
- Exercise vs control for depression, 25 RCTs: publication-bias-adjusted SMD 1.11 (95% CI 0.79–1.43). Figure verified only via a secondary copy of the full text; a published letter argues trim-and-fill was applied in the wrong direction and that reanalysis greatly reduces the effect. — [Schuch et al. 2016, J Psychiatr Res 77:42–51, PMID 26978184](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26978184/); critique: [letter summary](https://scoop-db.wp.hum.uu.nl/study/letter-to-the-editor-letter-to-the-editor-comment-on-schuch-et-al-exercise-as-a-treatment-for-depression-a-meta-analysis-adjusting-for-publication-bias/)
- Exercise in adults with diagnosed anxiety/stress-related disorders, 6 RCTs, n=262: SMD −0.582 (moderate), p=0.02. The CI printed in one catalogue record (−1.0 to −0.76) is impossible given the point estimate — CI unverified. — [Stubbs et al. 2017, Psychiatry Res 249:102–108, PMID 28088704](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28088704/) (doi 10.1016/j.psychres.2016.12.020)
- Single bout of exercise → state anxiety, 36 RCTs: Hedges g = 0.16 (SE 0.06), p<0.05; heterogeneous; moderated by intensity, modality, sex, health status, control condition; floor effects because samples start with low anxiety. Verified. — [Ensari et al. 2015, Depress Anxiety 32(8):624–634, PMID 25899389](https://www.pubmed.gov/25899389/) (doi 10.1002/da.22370)
- Single bout of aerobic exercise → positive activated affect: 158 studies, 450 effect sizes, N=13,101; mean corrected effect 0.47 (0.47 confirmed via secondary citations only); effect lasts at least ~30 min post-exercise; control conditions lower PAA. — [Reed & Ones 2006, Psychol Sport Exerc](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-acute-aerobic-exercise-on-positive-activated-affect/); 0.47 via [Leeds Beckett eprint](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/1426/10/Acute%20Effects%20of%20Aerobic%20Exercise%20on%20Feelings%20of%20Energy%20in%20Relation%20to%20Age%20and%20Gender.pdf)
- Single bout → energy vs fatigue: 16 studies, 678 participants; energy Δ = 0.47 (95% CI 0.39–0.56), homogeneous; fatigue Δ = 0.03 (95% CI −0.08 to 0.13), i.e. no average change. Fatigue falls only when the energy gain is at least moderate, after low-to-moderate intensity lasting >20 min. Verified. — [Loy, O'Connor & Dishman 2013, Fatigue: Biomed Health Behav](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/21641846.2013.843266) (doi 10.1080/21641846.2013.843266)
- Chronic exercise → energy/fatigue, 70 RCTs, 6,807 subjects: combined improvement 0.37 SD (energy and fatigue pooled into one effect). — [Puetz, O'Connor & Dishman 2006, Psychol Bull 132(6):866–876; press summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/11/061101151005.htm). 2022 update separating the two: fatigue g = −0.374, energy g = 0.415. — [Wender, Manninen & O'Connor 2022, Front Psychol](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.907637/full)
- 10-min brisk walk vs candy bar, n=18, 12 test days, within-subject: walking gave significantly higher self-rated energy and lower tension for 2 h; sugar gave a brief energy lift then more tiredness and higher tension at 1 h. No effect sizes in abstract. — [Thayer 1987, J Pers Soc Psychol, PMID 3820066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3820066/)
- Four experiments on 10–15 min walks: consistent shift to higher activation and more positive valence; recovery returns toward calmness/relaxation. — [Ekkekakis, Hall, VanLanduyt & Petruzzello 2000, J Behav Med 23(3):245–275, PMID 10863677](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10863677/). Companion 10-min self-paced outdoor walk: benefit vs control dissipated by 10 min post-walk. — [Van Landuyt et al. abstract](https://publish.illinois.edu/exppl-kch-ahs/positive-affective-changes-following-a-10-minute-outdoor-walk/)
- Anger: meta-analysis of 154 studies (184 samples, 10,189 participants). Arousal-increasing activities (e.g. jogging/running, venting, hitting a bag) had no overall effect on anger/aggression: g = −0.02 [−0.13, 0.09], heterogeneous. Authors: findings "do not support the ideas that venting anger or going for a run are effective anger management activities." Verified from abstract. — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024, Clin Psychol Rev, PMID 38518585](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/)

**High-intensity exercise (HIIT)**
- 58 RCTs: HIIT vs non-active control improved mental well-being (SMD 0.418, 95% CI 0.135–0.701, n=12) and depression severity (SMD −0.496, 95% CI −0.973 to −0.020, n=10); vs active controls the well-being gain was smaller (SMD 0.229). — [Martland et al. 2022, Br J Sports Med 56(5):279, PMID 34531186](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34531186/)
- 34 RCTs: HIIT vs control for depressive symptoms SMD = −0.40; not different from other exercise (SMD −0.15, ns). — [Tao et al. 2024, J Psychosom Res, PMID 38603999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38603999/)
- Affect during exercise falls above the ventilatory/lactate threshold (see Q4). — [Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello 2011, Sports Med 41(8):641–671](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-pleasure-and-displeasure-people-feel-when-they-exercise-at-d/23076908)

**Resistance training**
- Anxiety: 16 articles, 31 effects, 922 participants; Δ = 0.31 (95% CI 0.17–0.44), I² = 28.3%; larger in healthy participants (Δ = 0.50) than in those with physical/mental illness (Δ = 0.19). Verified. — [Gordon et al. 2017, Sports Med, PMID 28819746](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28819746/)
- Depressive symptoms: 33 RCTs, 1,877 participants; Δ = 0.66 (95% CI 0.48–0.83); smaller in trials with blinded allocation/assessment. Verified. — [Gordon et al. 2018, JAMA Psychiatry](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/678d2a9c-4a2d-4cb3-9170-807faced6055)

**Yoga**
- Depression: 12 RCTs, 619 participants; yoga vs usual care SMD −0.69 (95% CI −0.99 to −0.39), moderate evidence; only 3 RCTs low risk of bias; smaller effects vs relaxation and aerobic exercise. Verified. — [Cramer et al. 2013, Depress Anxiety 30(11):1068–1083, PMID 23922209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23922209/)
- Anxiety: 8 RCTs, 319 participants; yoga vs no treatment SMD −0.43 (95% CI −0.74 to −0.11); vs active comparators SMD −0.86; authors call evidence for diagnosed anxiety disorders inconclusive. Verified. — [Cramer et al. 2018, Depress Anxiety 35(9):830–843](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/yoga-for-anxiety-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomi/)
- Stress physiology: 42 RCTs vs active controls; yoga asana reduced cortisol (evening/waking), systolic BP, resting heart rate and changed HRV; no clear effect on IL-6/IL-8/CRP. A Japanese evidence summary notes inconsistencies between abstract and body text. — [Pascoe, Thompson & Ski 2017, Psychoneuroendocrinology 86:152–168](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/40188/) (doi 10.1016/j.psyneuen.2017.08.008); caveat: [eJIM summary](https://www.ejim.mhlw.go.jp/doc/pdf/yoga/SR-sonota-5.pdf)
- Single yoga session → anxiety: meta-analysis of 30 studies (adults, incl. clinical), standardized mean effect 0.32 (95% CI 0.16–0.48), I² = 82.7%; single-session tai chi showed no anxiety effect; qigong estimate rested on very few studies. Journal/year not confirmed in this session (Dishman-group paper). — [abstract via Bohrium scholar record](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/iE4849406/Rod_K._Dishman)
- Yoga style matters acutely: small pilot found state anxiety fell after a meditative (Hatha-style) session but not after a power (Vinyasa-style) session. — [PMC7503485](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7503485)
- Anger: arousal-decreasing activities (slow-flow yoga, mindfulness, progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, timeout) reduced anger and aggression, g = −0.63 [−0.82, −0.43], robust across students/non-students, offenders/non-offenders. Verified. — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/); activity examples: [ScienceDaily summary of OSU release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm)

**Tai chi / qigong**
- Tai chi, 33 randomized and non-randomized trials (21 reported significant gains): pooled Hedges g 0.66 stress, 0.66 anxiety, 0.56 depression, 0.45 mood; trial quality modest. — [Wang C et al. 2010, BMC Complement Altern Med, PMID 20492638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20492638/)
- Qigong in healthy adults (RCTs): anxiety SMD −0.75, perceived stress SMD −0.88; few trials with methodological flaws. — [Wang CW et al. 2014, BMC Complement Altern Med 14:8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3893407) (doi 10.1186/1472-6882-14-8)
- Qigong, mixed populations: depressive symptoms SMD −0.75 vs waitlist/usual care; anxiety benefit not supported. — [Wang CW et al. 2013, PMC3671670](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3671670/)
- Wang F et al. 2014 (Int J Behav Med 21(4):605–617; 37 RCTs + 5 quasi-experimental) exists, but the depression "effect size" −5.97 in the record is not a plausible standardized effect — do not cite the number. — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/40511)
- Acute: single-session tai chi showed no effect on anxiety in the single-session meta-analysis above. — [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/iE4849406/Rod_K._Dishman)

**Stretching**
- No meta-analysis or acute RCT of stretching for mood was found. Stretching is frequently the *control* arm in exercise trials. A Spanish trial (134 office workers; 10-min daily desk stretching for 3 months; journal Atención Primaria) reported lower anxiety, pain and exhaustion and higher vitality — confirmed only through news coverage, original paper not seen. — [news coverage](https://runnersworldonline.com.au/workplace-stretching-eases-anxiety/)
- A 2024 meta-analysis in postmenopausal women cites an earlier finding that yoga improved depression whereas stretching did not (second-hand). — [BMC Public Health 2024](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12889-024-19348-2.pdf)

**Dancing**
- 41 controlled studies (N=2,374; 21 dance movement therapy, 20 dance): overall d = 0.60, I² = 72.6%; outcome clusters d = 0.53–0.85; DMT d = 0.30, dance interventions d = 0.81 (I² = 78%); larger effects on observational measures suggest bias. Depression/anxiety-specific values not confirmed. — [Koch et al. 2019, Front Psychol 10:1806; PEDro abstract](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/61819) (doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01806)
- 27 studies comparing structured dance (≥4 weeks) with other physical activity: dance ≥6 weeks is equivalent to other exercise for psychological/cognitive outcomes; preliminary evidence dance is superior for motivation, aspects of memory, social cognition and reducing distress, and *inferior* for stress, self-efficacy and language fluency. — [Fong Yan et al. 2024, Sports Med 54(5):1179–1205](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-023-01990-2.pdf) (doi 10.1007/s40279-023-01990-2). The university press release lists self-efficacy as a dance strength, contradicting the abstract. — [Univ. Sydney release](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/02/12/dancing-may-be-better-than-other-exercise-for-improving-mental-h.html)

**Walking in nature / green exercise**
- 90-min nature walk vs 90-min urban walk, 38 healthy adults: nature walk lowered self-reported rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activity; urban walk did not. — [Bratman et al. 2015, PNAS 112(28):8567–8572, PMID 26124129](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26124129)
- Pooled analysis of 10 UK studies, N=1,252: green exercise improved self-esteem and mood; the largest change came from the first 5 minutes, with diminishing returns; water presence gave larger effects; larger gains in people with mental illness. — [Barton & Pretty 2010, Environ Sci Technol; summary](https://travelwest.info/?p=1652). Effect sizes (self-esteem d = 0.46, mood d = 0.54) are **[unverified — recalled]**. Uncontrolled pre–post designs.
- Outdoor vs indoor exercise, 11 studies, 833 adults: outdoors gave greater revitalization and positive engagement, less tension, confusion, anger and depression, more energy; authors stress a paucity of high-quality evidence. — [Thompson Coon et al. 2011, Environ Sci Technol](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/766/). Update: acute outdoor green exercise may favourably influence affective valence and enjoyment "but not emotion, perceived exertion, exercise intensity, and biological markers." — [Lahart et al. 2019 (authorship inferred), PMC6518264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6518264)
- Nature-based outdoor activities, 50 studies (16 RCTs): RCT meta-analysis found improved depressive mood, reduced anxiety, improved positive affect and reduced negative affect; best programmes 8–12 weeks with 20–90 min sessions. Exact SMDs not verified (snippet had transcription errors). — [Coventry et al. 2021, SSM Popul Health 16:100934](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/178622)

**Forest bathing (shinrin-yoku)**
- 22 studies in review, 8 in meta-analysis: salivary cortisol lower in forest vs urban groups, MD = −0.05 µg/dl (95% CI −0.06 to −0.04), I² = 88%. Cortisol was *also* lower in forest groups before the intervention, suggesting anticipation/placebo effects. — [Antonelli, Barbieri & Donelli 2019, Int J Biometeorol 63(8):1117–1134, PMID 31001682](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31001682/) (doi 10.1007/s00484-019-01717-x)
- 20 studies on depression, anxiety, stress, anger: shinrin-yoku "can be effective in reducing mental health symptoms in the short term, particularly anxiety"; few RCTs (6 for depression, 5 for anxiety); pooled effect sizes not confirmed here. — [Kotera, Richardson & Sheffield 2022, Int J Ment Health Addict 20:337–361](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-020-00363-4) (doi 10.1007/s11469-020-00363-4)
- A 2026 Frontiers review cautions forest bathing should be seen as complementary health promotion, not therapy for mental disorders. — [Front Psychol 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1707829)

**Blue space**
- Systematic review of 35 quantitative studies: consistent positive associations between blue-space exposure and mental health/well-being (12 studies) and physical activity; mostly cross-sectional, causality unclear. — [Gascon et al. 2017, Int J Hyg Environ Health; BlueHealth summary](https://bluehealth2020.eu/?p=831)
- In Barton & Pretty's green-exercise analysis, sites with water gave larger mood/self-esteem gains. — [summary](https://travelwest.info/?p=1652)
- A 2024 meta-analysis of 47 studies found results vary significantly, especially for depression. — [MDPI Sustainability review citing it](https://mdpi.com/2071-1050/17/8/3749)

**Gardening**
- Meta-analysis of 22 studies (74–76 comparisons; sources differ): significant positive effect on health outcomes including reduced depression and anxiety and increased life satisfaction, quality of life, sense of community; publication bias detected but effect survives trim-and-fill; largest gains in patients/horticultural therapy. Overall effect 0.42 is **[unverified]**. Not restricted to RCTs. — [Soga, Gaston & Yamaura 2017, Prev Med Rep 5:92–99, PMC5153451](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5153451) (doi 10.1016/j.pmedr.2016.11.007)
- Acute RCT: 30 allotment gardeners, Stroop stressor then 30 min outdoor gardening vs indoor reading. Cortisol fell in both but significantly more with gardening; positive mood was fully restored after gardening and deteriorated further during reading. — [Van den Berg & Custers 2011, J Health Psychol 16(1):3–11](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/gardening-promotes-neuroendocrine-and-affective-restoration-from-/) (doi 10.1177/1359105310365577)

**Viewing nature / sitting outdoors**
- "Nature pill": 36 urban dwellers, 8 weeks, ≥10 min nature experience ≥3×/week, self-chosen time/place. Salivary cortisol dropped 21.3%/hour beyond the 11.7%/hour diurnal decline; efficiency greatest between 20 and 30 min; **activity type (sitting vs walking) did not change the cortisol response**. Alpha-amylase dropped 28.1%/h only in those sitting or sitting with some walking. — [Hunter, Gillespie & Chen 2019, Front Psychol 10:722, PMC6458297](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458297) (doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00722)
- Scoping review, 14 studies (10 from Japan), college-aged: as little as 10 min sitting or walking in nature improved psychological and physiological markers; doses tested 10–50 min; outcomes included lower heart rate/cortisol, more vigor and calm. — [Meredith et al. 2020, Front Psychol 10:2942, PMC6970969](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6970969) (doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02942)
- Meta-analysis, 32 studies, 2,356 participants: nature exposure produced a moderate increase in positive affect and a smaller, consistent decrease in negative affect; moderated by exposure type (real vs simulated), measure, location, age. r-values (≈0.31 PA, ≈−0.12 NA) are **[unverified — recalled]**. — [McMahan & Estes 2015, J Posit Psychol 10(6):507–519](https://core.ac.uk/download/228821655.pdf) (doi 10.1080/17439760.2014.994224)
- Weekly dose: 19,806 English adults, cross-sectional; ≥120 min/week in nature vs none: adjusted OR 1.59 (1.31–1.92) for good health and 1.23 (1.08–1.40) for high well-being at 120–179 min; no benefit below 120 min; peak 200–300 min; one long or several short visits equivalent. — [White et al. 2019, Sci Rep 9:7730; summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2019/06/spending-two-hours-a-week-in-nature-is-linked-to-better-health-and-well-being/) (doi 10.1038/s41598-019-44097-3)
- Loneliness: smartphone EMA, 756 participants, 16,602 assessments: overcrowding associated with higher momentary loneliness; contact with nature and social inclusivity with lower loneliness (robust to demographics). The "28% lower" figure appears in news coverage only. Observational. — [Hammoud et al. 2021, Sci Rep 11, PMC8688521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8688521) (doi 10.1038/s41598-021-03398-2)

**Sunlight / daylight**
- UK Biobank, ~502,000 adults, cross-sectional + longitudinal: each additional hour/day outdoors in daylight associated with lower odds of lifetime MDD (OR ~0.92–0.98), low mood (OR ~0.87–0.90), anhedonia (OR ~0.93–0.96), frequent tiredness (OR ~0.80–0.82), insomnia symptoms (OR ~0.94–0.97), and higher odds of happiness (OR ~1.41–1.48) and ease of getting up (OR ~1.46–1.49). Observational; light at the eye not measured; longitudinal effects smaller. — [Burns et al. 2021, J Affect Disord; AJMC summary](https://www.ajmc.com/view/how-daytime-light-exposure-affects-sleep-mood-related-outcomes) (doi 10.1016/j.jad.2021.08.056)

**Cold-water face immersion / cold exposure**
- Cold face test (cold stimulus to face) during a lab psychosocial stressor (MIST), 28 healthy adults, randomized between-subjects: cold-face group showed better HR/HRV recovery and a significantly lower cortisol response. Small; outcome is physiological stress, not self-reported mood. — [Richer et al. 2022, Sci Rep, PMC9649023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9649023/)
- 5-min head-out immersion at ~20 °C, 39 adults, **no control group**: increased positive affect (active, alert, attentive, proud, inspired) and reduced distress/nervousness, correlated with fMRI connectivity changes. — [Yankouskaya et al. 2023, Biology](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/short-term-head-out-whole-body-cold-water-immersion-facilitates-p/)
- Systematic review/meta-analysis, 11 RCTs, 3,177 participants: stress reduced 12 h post-immersion but **not** immediately or at 1 h; sleep quality and quality of life improved; **no significant effect on mood**. — [Cain et al. 2025, PLOS ONE, PMC11778651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11778651/) (doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0317615)

**Napping / rest**
- Meta-analysis in physically active adults/athletes: after normal sleep, napping reduced fatigue (SMD 0.91) and total mood disturbance (SMD 0.61) and improved perceived recovery (SMD 1.66); sleepiness ns; no significant benefit after sleep deprivation; certainty low to very low. — [Boukhris et al., Biol Sport 2026 (online 2025)](https://www.termedia.pl/doi/10.5114/biolsport.2026.153310)
- Umbrella review of 16 meta-analyses: naps <60 min maximize cognitive benefit (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.37–1.00) and reduce fatigue. — [Public Health Reviews 2026](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/public-health-reviews/articles/10.3389/phrs.2026.1609013/full)
- Older meta-analysis: nap benefit depends on nap length and time since nap. — [Driskell & Mullen 2005, Hum Factors, PMID 16170944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16170944/)

**Stair-climbing / very short movement breaks**
- 18 sleep-deprived college women, crossover: 10 min of low-to-moderate stair walking gave a larger increase in feelings of energy/vigor than 50 mg caffeine or placebo; effect was transient (immediately post); no effect on working memory, attention or other mood states. — [Randolph & O'Connor 2017, Physiol Behav; UGA release](https://coe.uga.edu/news/2017-04-skip-the-soda-opt-for-the-stairs-to-feel-more-energized/) (doi 10.1016/j.physbeh.2017.03.013)
- Randomized crossover, 30 sedentary adults, 6 h sitting days: both one 30-min morning walk and six hourly 5-min moderate walking "microbouts" raised self-rated energy and vigor vs sitting (p<0.05); only microbouts improved mood, reduced fatigue and food cravings at end of day. — [Bergouignan et al. 2016, Int J Behav Nutr Phys Act 13:113, PMID 27809874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27809874/) (doi 10.1186/s12966-016-0437-z)

### Inferences
- For "right now" suggestions, the acute literature (Ensari, Reed & Ones, Loy, Thayer, Ekkekakis, Hunter, Meredith, Van den Berg) is the directly relevant evidence; the large depression/anxiety meta-analyses (Singh, Noetel, Cramer, Gordon) test multi-week programmes, mostly in clinical or symptomatic samples, and should be labelled as supporting evidence for the *habit*, not the single session.
- The emotion-to-activity mapping the evidence supports best: low energy/fatigue → 10-min brisk walk, stairs, hourly 5-min movement; stress/tension → 10–30 min nature sit or walk, gardening, gentle yoga; anger → low-arousal activity (slow yoga, breathing, nature sit), not a run; rumination → nature walk (longer dose tested); low mood → walk/jog, yoga, strength, dance (programme-level evidence) plus green exercise for an immediate mood lift; anxiety → gentle yoga, qigong, light-to-moderate aerobic.
- "Fatigue" needs splitting: exercise reliably raises *energy* acutely but does not reduce *fatigue* on average unless the bout is low-to-moderate and >20 min.

### Gaps
- Boredom: no acute experiment of any physical/nature activity on state boredom was found.
- Loneliness: only observational/EMA evidence (Hammoud 2021; a 2024 Nature Mental Health EMA study showing lower well-being when alone was offset when physically active — [RUB release](https://news.rub.de/english/press-releases/2024-02-19-sports-science-physical-activity-counteracts-negative-consequences-being-alone)). No intervention meta-analysis located.
- Overwhelm: not measured as a construct in this literature; stress/tension is the nearest proxy.
- Stretching, acute dance, acute resistance exercise, and experimental (not observational) daylight exposure: no verified acute mood evidence obtained.
- Weinstein et al. 2024 (Psychosom Med; acute exercise → anxiety g = 0.497, 95% CI 0.263–0.730, 103 studies) surfaced in a search summary but no source URL was captured — treat as unverified.
- Unverified numbers needing a full-text check: Noetel per-modality g values; Barton & Pretty d values; McMahan & Estes r values; Soga 0.42; Stubbs CI; Coventry SMDs; Kotera pooled effects.

## Q2. Acute (single-bout) effects: what minimum dose works?

### Takeaway
The smallest tested doses with positive results are 5 minutes (green exercise; hourly walking microbouts), 10 minutes (brisk walk, stair walking, sitting/walking in nature), and 20–30 minutes (most efficient cortisol reduction from nature; threshold for fatigue reduction from exercise). Rumination evidence is at 90 minutes only.

### Cited Findings
- 5 min: largest marginal gain in mood and self-esteem from green exercise occurred in the first 5 minutes (pooled pre–post data, N=1,252). — [Barton & Pretty 2010 summary](https://travelwest.info/?p=1652)
- 5 min × 6 (hourly): improved energy, vigor, mood and reduced fatigue across a sitting day. — [Bergouignan et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27809874/)
- 10 min: brisk walk raised energy and lowered tension for up to 2 h. — [Thayer 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3820066/)
- 10–15 min: walks shift affect toward activation and pleasant valence; one study found the benefit gone by 10 min after stopping. — [Ekkekakis et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10863677/); [Van Landuyt et al.](https://publish.illinois.edu/exppl-kch-ahs/positive-affective-changes-following-a-10-minute-outdoor-walk/)
- 10 min: stair walking raised energy immediately, transiently. — [Randolph & O'Connor 2017](https://coe.uga.edu/news/2017-04-skip-the-soda-opt-for-the-stairs-to-feel-more-energized/)
- 10 min (range 10–50): sitting or walking in nature improved psychological/physiological markers in students. — [Meredith et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6970969)
- 20–30 min: most efficient window for cortisol reduction from a nature experience; benefits continue more slowly after. — [Hunter et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458297)
- 21–40 min moderate aerobic (typical of included studies): energy Δ = 0.47; fatigue reduced only with >20 min low-to-moderate intensity. — [Loy et al. 2013](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/21641846.2013.843266)
- 30 min: gardening after a stressor restored positive mood and lowered cortisol more than reading. — [Van den Berg & Custers 2011](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/gardening-promotes-neuroendocrine-and-affective-restoration-from-/)
- 30 min: prerecorded online yoga session reduced anxiety vs information-only control. — [Temple CommonHealth study](https://tuljournals.temple.edu/index.php/commonhealth/article/download/558/411/2211)
- 90 min: nature walk reduced rumination; shorter doses not tested in that study. — [Bratman et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26124129)
- Post-exercise positive activated affect persists ≥30 min. — [Reed & Ones 2006 abstract record](https://www.infona.pl/resource/bwmeta1.element.elsevier-f2f1e1d4-399f-315f-aead-2ee42b5a532f)
- Weekly (not acute): 120 min/week threshold, any split. — [White et al. 2019](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2019/06/spending-two-hours-a-week-in-nature-is-linked-to-better-health-and-well-being/)
- Cold-water immersion: no stress reduction immediately or at 1 h; effect only at 12 h. — [Cain et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11778651/)

### Inferences
- A 5–30 min app activity window fits the evidence for energy, tension/stress and positive affect. For rumination, a 10–30 min nature walk is an extrapolation below the tested 90-min dose and should be flagged as such.
- Acute affective benefits are short-lived (minutes to ~2 h), so claims should be "feel better now", not lasting change.

### Gaps
- No dose-finding RCT directly compares 5 vs 10 vs 20 vs 30 min for any emotion outcome; minimum doses come from the shortest dose each study happened to test.

## Q4. Does intensity matter for which emotion? Self-selected vs prescribed intensity

### Takeaway
Yes. For anger, raising arousal (running, venting) does not help and calming activity does. For in-the-moment pleasure, intensity above the ventilatory threshold reliably feels worse, and letting people choose their pace is the safer default; for depression at programme level, higher intensity appears somewhat more effective.

### Cited Findings
- Anger: arousal-decreasing g = −0.63 [−0.82, −0.43]; arousal-increasing g = −0.02 [−0.13, 0.09]; running and venting not supported. — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/)
- Within arousal-increasing activities, jogging was the one most likely to increase anger, while ball sports/playful physical activity tended to reduce it (reported in coverage and authors' commentary, not in the abstract). — [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/venting-doesnt-reduce-anger-but-something-else-does-says-study); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/effective-anger-reduction-techniques-revealed-in-comprehensive-review-of-154-studies/)
- Dual-mode evidence: pleasure is reduced mainly above the ventilatory/lactate threshold; below it most people feel better; near it responses vary widely between individuals; above it responses are homogeneously negative. Authors argue prescriptions should consider pleasure, not just efficacy and safety. — [Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello 2011, Sports Med 41(8):641–671](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-pleasure-and-displeasure-people-feel-when-they-exercise-at-d/23076908)
- Self-selected intensity is proposed to give more positive and less variable affect; people on average self-select around the ventilatory threshold. — [Rose & Parfitt 2010, IJBNPA](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-7-15)
- Depression (programmes): effects larger "particularly when intense". — [Noetel et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38355154/?dopt=Abstract)
- Fatigue: reductions only after low-to-moderate intensity >20 min. — [Loy et al. 2013](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/21641846.2013.843266)
- State anxiety: exercise intensity is a moderator of the acute effect (direction not captured). — [Ensari et al. 2015](https://www.pubmed.gov/25899389/)
- Yoga: meditative style reduced state anxiety acutely; power style did not (small pilot). — [PMC7503485](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7503485)
- Sprint intervals: affect declines more during 30 s and 15–20 s sprints than 5–6 s sprints (individual-participant meta-analysis). — [Metcalfe et al. 2022, cited record](https://stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/1797216)

### Inferences
- App rule: when anger (or high-arousal anxiety/agitation) dominates, rank low-arousal options (slow yoga, breathing-paced stretching, nature sit, slow walk) above running/HIIT. When low energy or low mood dominates, activating options (brisk walk, stairs, dance) are appropriate.
- Phrase prompts as "at a pace that feels good" rather than prescribing intensity; this matches the dual-mode evidence and reduces risk.
- A slow outdoor walk is plausibly anger-compatible (low arousal + nature), but no study in this set tested walking for anger directly.

### Gaps
- No verified meta-analysis of in-task affect in HIIT vs moderate continuous exercise was retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- The direction of the intensity moderator in Ensari 2015 was not captured.

## Q5. Contraindications and safety

### Takeaway
Light-to-moderate self-paced activity needs no screening for most adults; vigorous suggestions should be gated on being a regular exerciser with no cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease or symptoms, and avoided within about an hour of bedtime. Cold immersion and long naps carry their own cautions.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2015 screening model depends on (1) current activity level, (2) signs/symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, (3) desired intensity; risk-factor counting was dropped to reduce barriers. "Exerciser" = ≥30 min moderate activity ≥3 days/week for ≥3 months. Exact algorithm branches not verified here. — [Riebe et al. 2015, Med Sci Sports Exerc 47(11):2473–2479](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180) (doi 10.1249/MSS.0000000000000664)
- Evening exercise, 23 studies in healthy adults: exercise within 4 h of bed does not harm sleep and moderate exercise slightly increases deep sleep; exception — vigorous exercise ending ≤1 h before bedtime may impair sleep-onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency (based on one study; heart rate still >20 bpm above resting at bedtime). — [Stutz, Eiholzer & Spengler 2019, Sports Med 49(2):269–287; ETH release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/617842)
- Above-threshold intensity produces displeasure in most people, which matters for someone already distressed. — [Ekkekakis et al. 2011](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-pleasure-and-displeasure-people-feel-when-they-exercise-at-d/23076908)
- Running/arousing exercise is not an effective anger remedy. — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/)
- Cold-water immersion: mood benefit not supported by RCT meta-analysis; evidence base small. — [Cain et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11778651/)
- Naps: benefits concentrated in naps <60 min. — [Public Health Reviews 2026 umbrella review](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/public-health-reviews/articles/10.3389/phrs.2026.1609013/full)
- Forest bathing/nature should be positioned as complementary, not treatment for mental disorders. — [Front Psychol 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1707829)
- Most exercise-for-depression evidence is low/very-low certainty. — [Noetel et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38355154/?dopt=Abstract)

### Inferences
- Suggested app guardrails (judgment, not from a single source): default to light/moderate self-paced activity; show vigorous options only if the user reports being regularly active; add a "stop if chest pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness" line; suppress vigorous options in the hour before the user's bedtime; do not suggest cold immersion to people with heart conditions (cold-shock physiology — no peer-reviewed source captured in this session); keep naps short and early in the day; add a signpost to professional help for persistent low mood rather than implying an activity treats depression.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was retrieved in this session for: cold-shock cardiac risk, sleep inertia/nap timing thresholds, exercise during acute illness or pregnancy, or heat/UV cautions for sunlight exposure. These cautions in the dataset are marked as general guidance.
