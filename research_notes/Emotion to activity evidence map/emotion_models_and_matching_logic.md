## 1. Emotion taxonomy: which fixed set of classes?

### Takeaway
Use a hybrid: a small set of ~12 discrete labels (what the vision LLM emits and what users understand), each pinned to valence × arousal coordinates (what the matching rules operate on). The dimensional layer comes from Russell's circumplex and Thayer's energy/tension split; the labels are chosen to line up with validated self-report constructs (DASS stress/anxiety/depression, POMS anger/fatigue/vigor, PANAS PA/NA) so each class has a defensible meaning.

### Cited Findings
- **Russell circumplex**: affect words arrange around a circle defined by two bipolar dimensions, pleasure–displeasure and arousal–sleepiness — [Russell 1980, JPSP 39:1161–1178](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077714) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Circumplex as an integrative model**: every affective state is treated as a combination of valence and arousal systems rather than a discrete module — [Posner, Russell & Peterson 2005, Dev Psychopathol 17:715–734](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954579405050340) [PM: PMID 16262989; the abstract says all affective states arise from "two independent neurophysiological systems" and contrasts this with basic-emotion theories; it does not name valence and arousal].
- **Three-factor (PAD) norms**: pleasure, arousal and dominance ratings for 151 emotion terms — [Russell & Mehrabian 1977, J Res Pers 11:273–294](https://doi.org/10.1016/0092-6566(77)90037-X) [M; not in PubMed; term count unverified].
- **Warriner norms (opened as data)**: 13,915 English lemmas rated 1–9 on valence, arousal, dominance. Lexicon means computed from the file: valence M = 5.06 (SD 1.27), arousal M = 4.21 (SD 0.90). Selected rows (valence / arousal): tense 2.75 / 5.32; stress 1.79 / 4.72; anxious 3.80 / 6.20; nervous 3.56 / 5.51; worried 3.27 / 5.81; angry 2.53 / 6.20; sad 2.10 / 3.49; lonely 2.67 / 4.37; tired 4.29 / 3.67; fatigued 2.89 / 3.78; bored 2.95 / 3.65; restless 3.63 / 4.05; calm 6.89 / 1.67; relaxed 7.25 / 2.49; content 6.70 / 3.17; happy 8.47 / 6.05; energetic 7.57 / 6.10; excited 8.11 / 6.43; neutral 5.50 / 3.45 — [Warriner, Kuperman & Brysbaert 2013, Behav Res Methods 45:1191–1207](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-012-0314-x) [PM: PMID 23404613; DOI and "13,915 English lemmas" confirmed]; data file: [JULIELab/XANEW mirror](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JULIELab/XANEW/master/Ratings_Warriner_et_al.csv) [opened].
- **PANAS**: two 10-item scales for Positive Affect and Negative Affect; high PA = energetic, alert engagement; high NA = distress — [Watson, Clark & Tellegen 1988, JPSP 54:1063–1070](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.6.1063) [PM: PMID 3397865; "two 10-item mood scales" confirmed; the high-PA/high-NA gloss is not in the abstract]. Useful as an outcome measure, but PA and NA are both "activated" poles, so PANAS under-represents low-arousal states (calm, tired, bored) [M].
- **Ekman basic emotions** (anger, fear, sadness, disgust, happiness, surprise) — [Ekman 1992, Cogn Emot 6:169–200](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699939208411068) [M; not in PubMed — PubMed indexes only a different 1992 Ekman paper, "Are there basic emotions?", Psychol Rev 99:550–553, PMID 1344638]. The set omits the states a wellbeing app most needs (stress, fatigue, boredom, loneliness, calm).
- **Plutchik's wheel**: 8 primaries in opposing pairs with intensity gradations; adjacent primaries blend into "dyads" (e.g. joy + trust = love) — Plutchik 1980, *Emotion: A Psychoevolutionary Synthesis* (book, no DOI) [M]. This is the classic theoretical precedent for naming combinations, but the specific dyads are theory, not empirically validated blends [M].
- **Cowen & Keltner**: self-reports to 2,185 short videos yielded 27 distinct categories bridged by continuous gradients; categories, more than valence/arousal alone, organised the reports — [Cowen & Keltner 2017, PNAS 114:E7900–E7909](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1702247114) [PM: PMID 28874542; 2,185 videos, 27 varieties and the categories-over-valence/arousal finding all confirmed in the abstract]. Supports emitting several graded scores per input rather than one hard label.
- **POMS**: six mood factors — tension-anxiety, depression-dejection, anger-hostility, vigor-activity, fatigue-inertia, confusion-bewilderment — McNair, Lorr & Droppleman 1971, POMS manual (no DOI) [M]. It is the main validated source for "fatigue" and "vigor" as mood states separate from sadness and happiness.
- **DASS**: depression (low positive affect, anhedonia), anxiety (autonomic arousal, fear) and stress (tension, irritability, difficulty relaxing) are separable but correlated scales — [Lovibond & Lovibond 1995, Behav Res Ther 33:335–343](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(94)00075-U) [PM: PMID 7726811; non-clinical sample N = 717; the abstract confirms a three-scale factor structure and the DASS's use for discriminating depression, anxiety and tension/stress; the bracketed scale descriptions are not in the abstract]. This is the justification for keeping "stress" and "anxiety" as two classes.
- **Thayer's two arousal dimensions**: energetic arousal (energy ↔ tiredness) and tense arousal (tension ↔ calm), giving four moods: calm-energy, calm-tiredness, tense-energy, tense-tiredness — Thayer 1989, *The Biopsychology of Mood and Arousal* (OUP, book) [M]. Thayer, Newman & McClain state that their mood-regulation results "support a 2-dimensional biopsychological model of mood" — [Thayer, Newman & McClain 1994, JPSP 67:910–925](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910) [PM: PMID 7983582; the abstract ends "Results support a 2-dimensional biopsychological model of mood"].

### Inferences
- A single arousal axis cannot represent "stressed + tired" (it averages to the middle). Thayer's split solves this: keep **tension** and **energy** as two derived scores. Suggested derivation: `tension = max(stressed, anxious, angry, restless)`, `energy = max(energetic, happy·0.5) − max(tired, bored·0.5)`. This is a design choice, not a validated scoring key.
- Warriner arousal ratings are compressed (SD 0.90) and are ratings of *words*, not of felt states; "restless" (4.05) and "stress" (4.72) score lower than theory predicts. The taxonomy table therefore reports the norm-derived coordinate and flags where a theory-based adjustment is advisable.
- Recommended 12 classes: stressed, anxious, angry, sad, lonely, tired, bored, restless, calm, content/happy, energetic/excited, neutral. Disgust and surprise (Ekman) are dropped because no regulation activity maps onto them in this use case.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study validates this exact 12-class set, nor vision-LLM output against DASS/POMS/PANAS. The mapping of labels to constructs is by content, not by measured convergent validity.
- Russell & Mehrabian's term-level PAD values could not be opened, so Warriner is the only normative source used for coordinates.

## 2. Mixed emotions

### Takeaway
Co-occurring emotions are well documented, both opposite-valence blends (happy + sad) and same-valence clusters (stress + anxiety + low mood), so the recommender should treat the input as a vector and reason over the top two or three scores, not the argmax.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis of mixed-emotion inductions**: 63 experimental studies; overall effect d = 0.77 (random effects, described by the authors as moderate to high); the effect held across affect-model structures and types of mixed emotion; "minimum index" measures gave smaller effects, subjective measures larger — [Berrios, Totterdell & Kellett 2015, Front Psychol 6:428](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00428) [PM: PMID 25926805; 63 studies and d = 0.77 confirmed; the abstract adds that samples with more women showed larger effects].
- **Happy and sad at the same time**: people report both on emotionally complex occasions (bittersweet films, graduation, moving out) — [Larsen, McGraw & Cacioppo 2001, JPSP 81:684–696](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.684) [PM: PMID 11642354; film (*Life Is Beautiful*), moving out of dormitories and graduation confirmed in the abstract; PubMed lists no DOI for this record, so the DOI is unconfirmed]; later studies ruled out artefact explanations with continuous and simultaneous measures — [Larsen & McGraw 2011, JPSP 100:1095–1110](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021846) [PM: PMID 21219075].
- **Everyday frequency**: experience sampling of more than 11,000 smartphone users; people reported at least one emotion 90% of the time; joy, love and anxiety were most frequent; positive emotions were reported 2.5 times more often than negative ones, yet positive and negative emotions were "relatively frequently" experienced together — [Trampe, Quoidbach & Taquet 2015, PLoS ONE 10:e0145450](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0145450) [PM: PMID 26698124; all figures confirmed in the abstract]. A specific percentage for mixed moments could not be confirmed.
- **Emotion differentiation (granularity)**: people differ in how finely they distinguish same-valence states; higher differentiation is associated with better regulation and less maladaptive coping under intense distress — [Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight 2015, Curr Dir Psychol Sci 24:10–16](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721414550708) [M; not in PubMed]; original evidence that differentiating negative emotions predicts more regulation — [Barrett, Gross, Christensen & Benvenuto 2001, Cogn Emot 15:713–724](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699930143000239) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Anxiety + depression (tripartite model)**: shared general negative affect, plus low positive affect specific to depression and physiological hyperarousal specific to anxiety — [Clark & Watson 1991, J Abnorm Psychol 100:316–336](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.100.3.316) [PM: PMID 1918611; abstract wording is "general distress, physiological hyperarousal (specific anxiety), and anhedonia (specific depression)"].
- **Stress + anxiety + depression**: the three DASS scales are distinct but moderately intercorrelated — [Lovibond & Lovibond 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(94)00075-U) [PM for citation; inter-scale correlations are not in the abstract and remain not confirmed — the abstract only gives DASS Anxiety–BAI r = 0.81 and DASS Depression–BDI r = 0.74].
- **Negative emotions cluster in daily life**: same-valence emotions tend to co-occur; "connector" emotions such as joy promote same-valence and inhibit opposite-valence emotions — [Trampe et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0145450) [PM: the abstract's network analysis describes "connector emotions (e.g., joy), which stimulate same valence emotions while inhibiting opposite valence emotions"; it does not state that negative emotions cluster more than positive ones].

### Inferences
- Because negative states co-occur so often, a high score on two negative labels is the expected case, not an edge case; the app should have explicit combination rules (Section 4) rather than falling back to the top label.
- Showing the user the top two labels and letting them correct them ("Does *stressed and tired* sound right?") doubles as an emotion-labelling exercise, which the differentiation literature suggests is itself useful. That benefit is an extrapolation from correlational work, not a tested app feature.

### Gaps
- No source was confirmed this session for the specific pairs **anger + sadness**, **boredom + loneliness**, or **fatigue + low mood** (the search for boredom–loneliness correlations was cut off by the search limit). Fatigue is a recognised symptom of depression and POMS fatigue and depression scales correlate, but no citation was verified. Treat these pairings as plausible and common, not as cited facts.

## 3. Strategy–situation fit: what determines which strategy works?

### Takeaway
No strategy is best everywhere. The best-supported moderators are **emotional intensity** (disengage/distract when high, reappraise when low), **arousal direction** (calm down anger; activate low mood), **controllability** (reappraise what cannot be changed, act on what can), and **having several options** (flexibility).

### Cited Findings
- **Process model**: five families ordered by when they act — situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation — [Gross 1998, Rev Gen Psychol 2:271–299](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.3.271) [M; not in PubMed]; extended model adds identification, selection and implementation stages, so choosing the wrong strategy for the context is itself a failure point — [Gross 2015, Psychol Inq 26:1–26](https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Meta-analysis of experimental comparisons** (306 comparisons): cognitive change d+ = 0.36; response modulation d+ = 0.16; attentional deployment overall d+ = 0.00, but within it distraction d+ = 0.27 and concentration (focusing on the feeling) d+ = −0.26; suppressing expression d+ = 0.32, suppressing the experience d+ = −0.04; perspective-taking reappraisal d+ = 0.45 versus reappraising the emotional response d+ = 0.23 — [Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, Psychol Bull 138:775–808](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600) [PM: PMID 22582737; DOI and every number here confirmed in the PubMed abstract, which also gives reappraising the emotional stimulus d+ = 0.36 and suppressing thoughts of the event d+ = −0.12]. A published critique (Augustine & Hemenover 2013, PMID 23607433) questioned inclusion criteria and effect-size selection, and the authors replied — [Miles, Sheeran & Webb 2013 reply, PubMed 23607434](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23607434/) [PM: author order on the record is Miles, Sheeran, Webb; Psychol Bull 139:730–734].
- **Intensity moderates choice**: across three experiments (emotional pictures and electric stimulation), people chose reappraisal for low-intensity negative situations and distraction for high-intensity ones — [Sheppes, Scheibe, Suri & Gross 2011, Psychol Sci 22:1391–1396](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611418350) [PM: PMID 21960251; DOI and finding confirmed; the abstract itself says distraction "blocks emotional processing at an early stage before it gathers force" and reappraisal "allows emotional processing"]. The framework paper reports six studies on emotional, cognitive and motivational determinants of regulation choice — [Sheppes et al. 2014, J Exp Psychol Gen 143:163–181](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030831) [PM: PMID 23163767; the claim that reappraisal is costlier and less effective at high intensity is not in the abstract and is not confirmed].
- **Habitual strategies and psychopathology** (114 studies, 241 effect sizes): the abstract reports a large effect size for rumination, medium to large for avoidance, problem solving and suppression, and small to medium for reappraisal and acceptance (corrected in the PubMed pass: problem solving belongs in the medium-to-large group, not with reappraisal and acceptance) — [Aldao, Nolen-Hoeksema & Schweizer 2010, Clin Psychol Rev 30:217–237](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2009.11.004) [PM: PMID 20015584; strategy-level r values are not in the abstract and remain not confirmed].
- **Controllability moderates reappraisal** (N = 170): with uncontrollable stress, higher reappraisal ability went with lower depression; with controllable stress, higher reappraisal ability went with *higher* depression — [Troy, Shallcross & Mauss 2013, Psychol Sci 24:2505–2514](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613496434) [PM: PMID 24145331; N = 170 and both directions confirmed. PubMed lists an erratum: Psychol Sci 2016;27(3):428–431, doi 10.1177/0956797615627417 — not opened, so check it before quoting exact statistics].
- **Regulatory flexibility**: three components — context sensitivity, a repertoire of strategies, and responsiveness to feedback — [Bonanno & Burton 2013, Perspect Psychol Sci 8:591–612](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691613504116) [PM: PMID 26173226; the three components are confirmed in the abstract]; flexibility defined as variability in strategy use that tracks changes in the environment — [Aldao, Sheppes & Gross 2015, Cogn Ther Res 39:263–278](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-014-9662-4) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Anger and arousal** (154 studies, 184 samples, 10,189 participants): arousal-decreasing activities (the abstract names deep breathing, mindfulness, meditation) reduced anger and aggression, g = −0.63, [−0.82, −0.43]; arousal-increasing activities (the abstract names hitting a bag, jogging, cycling) were ineffective overall, g = −0.02 [−0.13, 0.09]; jogging tended to increase anger, while ball sports and PE classes reduced it (this last clause is from the press summary only, not the abstract) — [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024, Clin Psychol Rev 109:102414](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414) [PM: PMID 38518585; DOI, 154 studies / 184 samples / 10,189 participants and both effect sizes confirmed in the PubMed abstract, which labels the metric "g" (it does not spell out "Hedges"); activity-level results (jogging, ball sports, PE, yoga, relaxation) are only from the [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240318142352.htm)].
- **Venting**: hitting a punching bag while thinking of the provoker increased later anger and aggression compared with doing nothing — [Bushman 2002, Pers Soc Psychol Bull 28:724–731](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167202289002) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Behavioural activation** (scheduling pleasant, mastery and valued activities) is an effective treatment for depression — [Ekers et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9:e100100](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100) [PM: PMID 24936656; 26 RCTs, 1,524 subjects; post-treatment SMD −0.74 (CI −0.91 to −0.56; k = 25, N = 1,088) versus controls and −0.42 (CI −0.83 to −0.00; k = 4, N = 283) versus medication; study quality mostly low and follow-up short]; it also raises wellbeing in non-clinical samples — [Mazzucchelli, Kane & Rees 2010, J Posit Psychol 5:105–121](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760903569154) [PM: PMID 20539837; 20 randomised studies, 1,353 participants, pooled Hedges's g = 0.52 on wellbeing, found for non-clinical participants and for those with elevated depressive symptoms].
- **Opposite action**: act against the emotion's action urge when the emotion does not fit the facts (approach when afraid, get active when sad, gently avoid or be kind when angry) — Linehan 2015, *DBT Skills Training Manual*, 2nd ed., Guilford (book, no DOI) [M]. The same manual's high-distress skills (cold water on the face, intense exercise, paced breathing, muscle relaxation) are clinical practice with limited component-level trials [M].
- **Iso principle**: first match music to the current mood, then shift gradually toward the target mood — [Heiderscheit & Madson 2015, Music Ther Perspect 33:45–52](https://doi.org/10.1093/mtp/miu042) [M; not in PubMed; a case-study paper]. Controlled test: after a sadness induction, healthy adults heard two pieces in one of four orders (happy-happy, sad-sad, happy-sad, sad-happy); order affected emotional state and results were "generally consistent" with the iso principle, but not all group differences were significant — [Starcke, Mayr & von Georgi 2021, IJERPH 18:12486](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312486) [PM: PMID 34886210; the sad-then-happy group ended with higher positive affect and valence and lower negative affect, "not all the between-group differences reached significance"].
- **Mood self-regulation survey** (four studies of what people do to change a bad mood, raise energy and reduce tension): exercise was judged the most effective mood-regulating behaviour, and the best general strategy for a bad mood was a combination of relaxation, stress management, cognitive and exercise techniques — [Thayer, Newman & McClain 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910) [PM: PMID 7983582; confirmed in the PubMed abstract]. A later review quotes p. 921: exercise was self-rated most successful at changing a bad mood, fourth at raising energy, and third or fourth at reducing tension [V via secondary source]. Psychotherapists rated "active mood management" most effective for a bad mood [V via secondary]. Secondary summaries add that music did well in all three categories, social interaction helped mood and tension more than energy, and a "pep talk"/controlling thoughts ranked high for energy [V via a non-peer-reviewed summary only].
  - Strategy factors confirmed by a secondary paper: Active Mood Management; Seeking Pleasurable Activities and Distraction; Passive Mood Management (list truncated). Remaining factors recalled from memory: Social Support/Ventilation/Gratification; Direct Tension Reduction (drugs, alcohol, sex); Withdrawal-Avoidance [M, unverified]. Sample sizes per the PubMed abstract: Study 1 N = 102 (open-ended questionnaire); Studies 2 and 4 used one representative sample of N = 308; Study 3 used 26 psychotherapists [PM]. The recalled fourth figure of 128 does not appear in the abstract and was removed as not confirmed. The ranked strategy lists are not in the abstract and the paper is not in PMC, so the ranks and the factor names here remain unverified. Passive strategies (TV, eating, being alone) and direct tension reduction were rated least successful [M, unverified].
- **Brief walk raises energy**: a 10-minute brisk walk raised self-rated energy and lowered tension for up to two hours, whereas a sugar snack raised energy briefly and then increased tiredness and tension — [Thayer 1987, JPSP 52:119–125](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.1.119) [PM: PMID 3820066; 18 volunteers, 12 test days, candy bar versus a brisk 10-minute walk; walking gave reliable increases in energy and decreases in tension for 2 h; the snack gave significantly higher tension after 1 h and initially more energy followed 1 h later by more tiredness and less energy].
- **Classification of regulation strategies**: 162 distinct self-reported strategies; card-sort by 24 participants and cluster analysis supported distinctions between cognitive vs behavioural, diversion vs engagement, and active distraction vs direct avoidance, with lower-level groups for venting, reappraisal and seeking social support — [Parkinson & Totterdell 1999, Cogn Emot 13:277–303](https://doi.org/10.1080/026999399379285) [V; not in PubMed].

### Inferences
- The Parkinson–Totterdell 2×2 (cognitive/behavioural × diversion/engagement) is a ready-made tagging scheme for the activity catalogue; every activity should carry those two tags plus an **arousal effect** tag (raises / lowers / neutral) and an **effort** tag (low / medium / high).
- Intensity gating (distract first when scores are high) is directly supported for *choice* and laboratory *effectiveness* with pictures and shocks; applying it to real-life stress detected from a face or text is an extrapolation.
- Troy et al. implies the app should ask one question when stress is high: "Is this something you can do something about right now?" — yes → planning/problem-solving; no → reappraisal/acceptance/soothing.

### Gaps
- The full ranked lists in Thayer et al. (1994) Tables could not be opened (the PubMed pass found the abstract only; the paper is not in PMC); only the abstract-level and secondary-source rankings above are confirmed. Anyone citing ranks beyond "exercise first for bad mood" should check the paper.
- No meta-analysis was found that directly compares activity classes *by emotion combination*.

## 4. Rule set for combinations (derivation)

### Takeaway
Combination rules can be derived by composing four tested principles — arousal direction, intensity gating, controllability, and energy cost — but almost every *combination* rule is an extrapolation from single-emotion evidence. The Matching rules table at the end marks each rule accordingly.

### Cited Findings
- High-arousal negative → lower arousal first: [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414) [PM] (anger only).
- High intensity → attention-based or sensory strategies before cognitive ones: [Sheppes et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611418350) [PM].
- Low-arousal negative → activation: [Ekers et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100) [PM]; exercise as the top self-rated bad-mood strategy: [Thayer et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910) [PM].
- Avoid focusing on the feeling and venting: concentration d+ = −0.26 in [Webb et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600) [PM]; arousal-increasing activities ineffective overall in [Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414) [PM].
- Low-effort interventions are liked most, high-effort ones reduce stress most (four-week study, N = 86) — [Howe et al. 2022, CHI '22](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3502027) [V; not in PubMed]. This supports offering a low-effort option when the user is tired while keeping a higher-effort option available.
- Positive states → savour and share: telling others about positive events ("capitalization") is associated with higher positive affect beyond the event itself — [Gable, Reis, Impett & Asher 2004, JPSP 87:228–245](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228) [PM: PMID 15301629; four studies; the benefit was larger when others responded actively and constructively]; positive emotions broaden momentary thought–action repertoires and build lasting resources — [Fredrickson 2001, Am Psychol 56:218–226](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.56.3.218) [PM: PMID 11315248; a theory paper reviewing "preliminary empirical evidence"].
- Loneliness: interventions targeting maladaptive social cognition outperformed those simply increasing contact or social skills — [Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo 2011, Pers Soc Psychol Rev 15:219–266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394) [PM: PMID 20716644; the abstract says that among randomised comparison studies the most successful interventions addressed maladaptive social cognition, and that single-group and non-randomised designs gave larger effects; no effect sizes in the abstract].
- Boredom arises from an attention or meaning deficit (task too easy/too hard, or not meaningful), implying the fix is a better-matched challenge or a more meaningful activity — [Westgate & Wilson 2018, Psychol Rev 125:689–713](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000097) [PM: PMID 29963873; the MAC model: an attentional component (mismatch between cognitive demands and mental resources, under- or over-stimulation) and a meaning component (mismatch between activity and valued goals)]; boredom defined as "the aversive experience of wanting, but being unable, to engage in satisfying activity" — [Eastwood et al. 2012, Perspect Psychol Sci 7:482–495](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612456044) [PM: PMID 26168505; quote confirmed].
- Breathwork (deliberate control of the breath; the meta-analysis is not limited to slow breathing — corrected in the PubMed pass) is associated with lower self-reported stress than non-breathwork controls in randomised trials: g = −0.35 [95% CI −0.55, −0.14], k = 12, 785 adults, most studies at moderate risk of bias; anxiety g = −0.32 (k = 20) and depressive symptoms g = −0.40 (k = 18) — [Fincham et al. 2023, Sci Rep 13:432](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y) [PM: PMID 36624160].

### Inferences
Proposed decision procedure (design proposal, not a validated algorithm):
1. **Safety gate** (Section 6) before anything else.
2. **Select active emotions**: scores ≥ 0.4, keep the top two (three if close). If none, treat as neutral.
3. **Compute** `tension`, `energy`, `valence` (Section 1) and `intensity = max score`.
4. **Intensity gate**: if intensity ≥ 0.75 on a negative emotion, restrict the first suggestion to short sensory/attentional activities (breathing, grounding, a short walk, absorbing distraction); offer cognitive or social activities as the follow-up.
5. **Look up the combination rule** (table below); if no pair rule matches, fall back to the quadrant rule for the dominant emotion.
6. **Personalise within the allowed classes** (Section 5), then show two or three options and let the user pick.
7. **Ask for feedback** ("Did that help?") and feed it into the ranking — this implements the "feedback responsiveness" component of flexibility.

Tie-breaking principle for conflicting needs (e.g. tense + tired): satisfy the *tension* need first with a low-energy-cost activity, because arousal-increasing activities are not helpful for high-arousal negative states, and low-effort options are better accepted. This ordering is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- Thresholds (0.4, 0.75) are arbitrary engineering defaults; no study calibrates LLM emotion scores to felt intensity.
- No direct trials for: stressed + tired, anxious + lonely, angry + sad, bored + restless, bored + lonely as *pairs*.

## 5. Personalisation

### Takeaway
Preference matching reliably improves satisfaction and uptake and gives a small outcome benefit, so using hobbies and likes to choose *among evidence-compatible* activities is defensible; but the evidence that matching improves wellbeing in self-help activities specifically is weak, so preferences should rank options inside the rule-allowed set, never override the rule.

### Cited Findings
- **Positive-activity model**: activity features (dosage, variety), person features (motivation, effort) and person–activity fit moderate whether positive activities raise wellbeing; an ill-fitting activity may be ineffective or backfire — [Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013, Curr Dir Psychol Sci 22:57–62](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721412469809) [V for content; not in PubMed, so the DOI is still unconfirmed].
- **Preference-matched positive exercises** (127 undergraduates, four weeks): participants preferred the matched exercise significantly more and reported slightly larger wellbeing gains, but that gain was not statistically significant, and adherence did not differ — Schueller 2011, "To each his own well-being boosting intervention", J Posit Psychol 6:300–313 — [World Database of Happiness record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/to-each-his-own-well-being-boosting-intervention-using-preference-to-guide-selection-13175/) [V]. The 2010 paper named in the brief, "Preferences for positive psychology exercises" — [J Posit Psychol 5:192–203](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439761003790948) [M; not in PubMed] — could not be confirmed by search or in PubMed; a related dissertation study (N = 792) found three preference clusters of exercises — [Schueller dissertation](https://repository.upenn.edu/edissertations/392) [V].
- **Positive psychology interventions meta-analysis** (51 interventions, 4,266 people): mean r = .29 for wellbeing and r = .31 for depressive symptoms; self-selection into the intervention, depression status, age, format and duration moderated effects — [Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009, J Clin Psychol 65:467–487](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.20593) [PM: PMID 19301241; all numbers and moderators confirmed in the abstract]. Later reviewers noted that non-randomised studies were included and effects may be inflated [V].
- **Treatment preference meta-analyses**: clients given their preferred treatment or involved in shared decisions had better satisfaction, completion and clinical outcome; outcome effect d = .15 — [Lindhiem, Bennett, Trentacosta & McLear 2014, Clin Psychol Rev 34:506–517](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2014.06.002) [PM: PMID 25189522; 34 articles describing 32 trials; satisfaction ESd = .34, completion ESOR = 1.37 (ESd = .17), clinical outcome ESd = .15]. Accommodating preferences in psychotherapy: 53 studies and over 16,000 clients; fewer dropouts (OR = 1.79) and better outcomes (d = 0.28) than a non-preferred condition — Swift, Callahan, Cooper & Parkin 2018, J Clin Psychol 74:1924–1937 — [PubMed 30091140](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30091140/) [PM: DOI 10.1002/jclp.22680; corrected in the PubMed pass — the abstract says 53 studies, not 51, and gives OR = 1.79, so the brief's figures were right].
- **Autonomy and adherence** (66 studies): more self-determined motivation is consistently associated with exercise; identified regulation predicts short-term adoption, intrinsic motivation predicts long-term adherence; competence satisfaction also predicts participation; most studies were non-experimental — [Teixeira, Carraça, Markland, Silva & Ryan 2012, IJBNPA 9:78](https://doi.org/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78) [PM: PMID 22726453].
- **Self-selected exercise intensity**: pleasure is reduced mainly above the ventilatory or lactate threshold (pleasant changes below it for most people, large individual variability near it, uniformly negative changes above it); self-selected rather than imposed intensity "appears to foster greater tolerance to higher intensity levels" (corrected in the PubMed pass: the abstract does not say affect is more positive at self-selected intensity) — [Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello 2011, Sports Med 41:641–671](https://doi.org/10.2165/11590680-000000000-00000) [PM: PMID 21780850; review of 33 articles from 1999–2009].
- **Self-chosen music** evokes more intense and more positive emotions than experimenter-chosen music — [Liljeström, Juslin & Västfjäll 2013, Psychol Music 41:579–599](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735612440615) [M; not in PubMed].
- **Variety**: in the Hedonic Adaptation Prevention model, continued variety in the experiences that follow a positive life change (and continued appreciation of the change) forestalls the erosion of wellbeing gains; a three-wave, 3-month longitudinal study of 481 students gave "good support" for the model (corrected in the PubMed pass: the study concerns positive life changes, not ways of performing positive activities, so applying it to activity rotation is an extrapolation) — [Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2012, Pers Soc Psychol Bull 38:670–680](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212436400) [PM: PMID 22361725].
- **JITAI framework**: components are decision points, intervention options, tailoring variables and decision rules, serving proximal and distal outcomes — [Nahum-Shani et al. 2018, Ann Behav Med 52:446–462](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-016-9830-8) [PM: PMID 27663578 for citation and DOI; the component list is not in the abstract and is still from memory].
- **Contextual-bandit recommender for stress micro-interventions (PopTherapy)**: web apps repurposed as short stress tasks, chosen by a machine-learning recommender using context; after four weeks participants reported higher stress self-awareness, lower depression-related symptoms and new coping skills; those receiving ML suggestions without the option to pick another showed a *tendency* toward more constructive coping — [Paredes et al. 2014, PervasiveHealth '14](https://doi.org/10.4108/icst.pervasivehealth.2014.255070) [V; not in PubMed; small study, no effect sizes seen].
- **Timing and effort** (N = 86, four weeks): just-in-time prompts led to more completed interventions than pre-scheduled ones but no significant difference in stress reduction; low-effort interventions were rated highest, high-effort ones reduced stress most; users wanted some control over timing — [Howe et al. 2022, CHI '22](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3502027) [V; not in PubMed].
- **Micro-intervention concept**: "highly focused interventions delivered in the context of a person's daily life with little burden on the individual", used as building blocks of a larger therapeutic process ("digital micro intervention care") — [Baumel, Fleming & Schueller 2020, J Med Internet Res 22:e20631](https://doi.org/10.2196/20631) [PM: PMID 33118946; a conceptual paper; the "events" and "proximal goal" wording is not in the abstract].
- **IntelliCare**: a suite of 14 single-purpose, skills-based apps designed for frequent, short interactions; in a single-arm, coach-assisted 8-week pilot of 99 participants with elevated depression or anxiety, 90 completed and PHQ-9 and GAD-7 fell substantially (P < .001); mean use 195.4 app launches (SD 141), average 1.1 minutes each — [Mohr et al. 2017, J Med Internet Res 19:e10](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.6645) [PM: PMID 28057609; corrected in the PubMed pass: this was a coached single-arm pilot, not a field trial, and the hub app is not mentioned in the abstract].

### Inferences
- Ranking formula for options inside the rule-allowed classes (design proposal): `score = evidence_weight(class | emotion pattern) + w_like·matches_hobby − w_dislike·matches_dislike + w_hist·past_helpfulness − w_repeat·recently_shown`. Dislikes should act as a hard filter; the repeat penalty implements variety.
- Always show two or three options and let the user choose: this supports autonomy (Teixeira et al.), mirrors the preference meta-analyses, and matches Howe et al.'s finding that users want control.
- For a hackathon, a rule-based ranker with a feedback counter is enough; a contextual bandit (PopTherapy-style) is the natural upgrade but needs weeks of per-user data to beat rules, and PopTherapy's own advantage was only a tendency.
- When the user is tired, default to the low-effort option but keep one higher-effort option visible, since effort trades off liking against benefit (Howe et al.).

### Gaps
- No trial shows that matching activities to *hobbies* (as opposed to stated preference among offered exercises) improves momentary mood outcomes.
- The self-selected music citation (Liljeström et al. 2013) is not in PubMed and is still unverified. The exercise-intensity and variety citations were verified in the PubMed pass and reworded to match their abstracts.

## 6. Safety and ethics guardrails

### Takeaway
Present the app as a self-care suggestion tool, not as detection or diagnosis: app benefits are real but small, harms are under-reported, and inferring emotion from faces is scientifically contested, so the app must let users correct the label, must not diagnose, and must route severe distress to human help.

### Cited Findings
- **Inferring emotion from faces**: a review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* found that facial configurations are not reliable, specific or generalisable enough to be read as diagnostic of an emotional state regardless of context, person and culture; the authors recommend neutral terms such as "facial configuration" and warn against technology that claims to read emotions from faces — [Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019, PSPI 20:1–68](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930) [PM: PMID 31313636]; a corrigendum exists (10.1177/1529100619889954) [PM: listed on the PubMed record as an erratum, Psychol Sci Public Interest 2019;20(3):165–166].
- **App efficacy**: meta-analysis of 176 RCTs — small significant effects on depression (g = 0.28, NNT ≈ 11.5) and generalised anxiety (g = 0.26, NNT ≈ 12.4); larger effects against inactive controls, consistent with a "digital placebo"; mostly short-term follow-up — Linardon et al. 2024, World Psychiatry 23:139–149 — [university record with abstract](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/current-evidence-on-the-efficacy-of-mental-health-smartphoneapps-/) [V]; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.21183) [PM: PMID 38214614; depression N = 33,567, generalised anxiety N = 22,394; g and NNT values confirmed]. Earlier meta-analysis of 66 RCTs with the same direction of results (depressive symptoms g = 0.28, generalised anxiety g = 0.30, stress g = 0.35) — [Linardon et al. 2019, World Psychiatry 18:325–336](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20673) [PM: PMID 31496095].
- **Harms are poorly reported**: of 171 app trials, only 55 reported adverse events; pooled deterioration rate 6.7% (95% CI 4.3–10.1) across 13 app conditions, not different from controls (OR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.62–1.01); reporting detail was too thin to judge risk — [Linardon et al. 2024, npj Digit Med 7:363](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-024-01388-y) [PM: PMID 39695173].
- **Crisis handling in existing apps is often poor**: a systematic assessment of 69 depression and suicide-prevention apps found only 5 (7%) included all six guideline-recommended suicide-prevention strategies, and 6 (9%) gave an erroneous crisis helpline number, including two apps with more than a million downloads each — [Martinengo et al. 2019, BMC Med 17:231](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1461-z) [PM: PMID 31852455].
- **Digital psychiatry overview** (engagement, privacy, evidence and regulation challenges) — [Torous et al. 2021, World Psychiatry 20:318–335](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20883) [PM: PMID 34505369].

### Inferences
Recommended guardrails (design recommendations derived from the above; not themselves trial-tested):
- **Wording**: say "It looks like you might be feeling…" and always offer "That's not right — let me pick". Never "You are depressed/anxious". Never use clinical terms (depression, anxiety disorder) as output labels; "low/sad" and "anxious/worried" are states, not diagnoses.
- **User correction outranks the model**: a self-reported label replaces the inferred one (follows from Barrett et al.).
- **Crisis gate**: if any negative score is extreme (e.g. ≥ 0.9), or severe negative states persist across several sessions, or any free text mentions self-harm or hopelessness, skip the activity list and show support information: "If you're struggling or feel unsafe, please reach out to someone you trust or a local crisis line / emergency services now." Crisis numbers must be localised and checked by a human (Martinengo et al.).
- **Disclaimer**: "This app offers general wellbeing suggestions. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose or treat any condition. If your low mood or anxiety lasts more than two weeks or gets in the way of daily life, talk to a health professional."
- **Activity safety**: no alcohol, food-as-comfort or drug suggestions (rated least effective in the mood-regulation literature [M]); exercise suggestions should be self-paced; breathing exercises should avoid long breath-holds.
- **Privacy**: face images and emotion scores are sensitive data; process and discard images, store scores locally, and ask consent explicitly.
- **Honest claims**: describe benefits as "may help", citing small average effects.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found on the accuracy of vision-LLM emotion scores against self-report; Barrett et al. is relevant by analogy only.
- No validated threshold exists for when an app-detected score warrants crisis escalation; the gate above is a precaution, not a screening tool.
