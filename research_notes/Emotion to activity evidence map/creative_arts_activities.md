## Q1. For each activity, which emotions does it measurably improve, with what effect size and study type?

### Takeaway
Only three activity families have meta-analytic support with quantified effects in mostly non-clinical adults: **music listening** (stress d ≈ 0.38 physiological / 0.55 psychological; anxiety d ≈ −0.30 to −0.77), **best-possible-self writing** (positive affect d ≈ 0.51) and **expressive writing** (very small, delayed: r ≈ .075; g ≈ −0.12). Visual art-making (drawing, colouring, clay, collage, painting) is supported by a consistent set of small single-session experiments, mostly on undergraduates, showing reduced state anxiety / negative mood, with the reliable finding that drawing **to distract or to depict something positive** beats drawing **to vent**. Knitting, cooking, photography, poetry, reading fiction and everyday creativity rest on observational or preliminary evidence.

### Cited Findings

#### General art-making (mixed media: drawing/collage/clay/painting)
- Kaimal, Ray & Muniz (2016), *Art Therapy* 33(2): quasi-experimental one-group pre/post, 39 healthy adults, 45 min of free art-making (collage, clay, markers); salivary cortisol significantly lower after the session; no significant differences by prior art experience, media choice or gender. No control group. [abstract-verified] — [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5004743); [Drexel repository](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Reduction-of-Cortisol-Levels-and-Participants/991019168497004721)
- The widely quoted "about 75% of participants had lower cortisol" figure comes from the authors via the university press release, not the abstract; just under half of participants reported limited art experience. — [Drexel news](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2016/June/Art_Hormone_Levels_Lower)
- Sandmire, Gorham, Rankin & Grimm (2012), *Art Therapy* 29(2):68–73: RCT, 57 undergraduates one week before final exams; art-making group chose among mandala painting/colouring, free painting, collage, still-life drawing, clay; state anxiety (STAI) fell significantly in the art group, no change in control. [abstract-verified] Session length (I recall 30 min) is **[unverified]**. — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2012.683748](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.683748); [UNE news](https://une.edu/news/2012/david-sandmire-sarah-gorham-nancy-rankin-and-david-grimm-publish-article-art-therapy)
- Joschko et al. (2024), *JAMA Network Open*: systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs of *active visual art therapy* (therapist-led; mostly clinical populations): 50 studies, 217 outcomes, 2,766 participants; pooled change-from-baseline SMD = 0.38 (95% CI 0.26–0.51), post-test SMD = 0.19 (0.12–0.26); improvement in only 18% of the 217 outcomes vs controls, 81% no difference; most outcomes were depression/anxiety; study quality mostly low, evidence "markedly heterogeneous". [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.28709](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393726)
- A 2026 meta-analysis of art therapy in adults *with depression* (13 RCT/quasi-experimental studies) reported SMD ≈ −0.69 for depressive symptoms and a small, non-significant effect on anxiety (3 studies), with substantial heterogeneity. Details from a search summary only. **[unverified beyond summary]** — [abstract listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2026/journal-article-abstracts/jpm-70127/)
- Fancourt & Finn (2019), WHO Health Evidence Network synthesis report 67: scoping review of 900+ publications (200+ reviews/meta-analyses, 3,000+ studies), concluding the arts contribute to prevention, promotion, management and treatment across the lifespan. It is a scoping review: no pooled effect sizes and no formal quality grading, so it supports "arts engagement is associated with wellbeing" but not any specific emotion→activity effect size. — [WHO report listing](https://www.jameelartshealthlab.org/research/research-projects/2019-who-report); [publisher record](https://styluspub.presswarehouse.com/browse/book/9789289054553/What-is-the-evidence-on-the-role-of-the-arts-in-improving-health-and-well-being)

#### Drawing / sketching (distract vs vent)
- Dalebroux, Goldstein & Winner (2008), *Motivation and Emotion*: after a negative mood induction, participants either drew their current mood (venting), drew something happy (positive emotion), or did a symbol-scanning control. Valence improved in all three; the largest gain followed the **positive-emotion drawing**; **venting was no better than the control task**; arousal unchanged. [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1)
- Drake & Winner (2012), *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* 6:251–261: Study 1 induced sadness with a film, Study 2 by recalling one's saddest experience; drawing something unrelated (a house) — distraction — improved mood more than drawing about the sad content (venting). [abstract-verified for design/finding; sample sizes and drawing minutes **unverified**] — [DOI 10.1037/a0026909](https://typeset.io/pdf/confronting-sadness-through-art-making-distraction-is-more-4yr4tzvok3.pdf)
- Drake, Coleman & Winner (2011), *Art Therapy* 28(1):26–30: 40 participants, sad mood induction, randomised to drawing vs writing; mood valence significantly more positive after drawing than writing, and more positive when participants reported using distraction rather than venting. [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2011.557032](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/07421656.2011.557032)
- Genuth & Drake (2021), *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*: 83 undergraduates, sadness **or anger** induction; drawing-to-distract gave greater mood improvement (PANAS) than drawing-to-express for both emotions. [abstract-verified; effect sizes not in abstract] — [DOI 10.1037/aca0000265](https://www.ovid.com/journals/paca/pdf/10.1037/aca0000265~the-benefits-of-drawing-to-regulate-sadness-and-anger)
- A further study of 160 adults (reported in a review; primary citation not confirmed) found distraction raised positive affect more than expression for drawing and thinking, and lowered negative affect more than expression across all four activities tested. **[secondary source]** — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10636421/)

#### Colouring (mandala / structured)
- Curry & Kasser (2005), *Art Therapy* 22(2): 84 undergraduates, anxiety induction, then 20 min [duration from memory, **unverified**] colouring a mandala, a plaid pattern, or a blank page; structured designs (mandala, plaid) reduced anxiety more than free-form colouring. Recorded as successfully replicated in the FORRT replication atlas. — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2005.10129441](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/07421656.2005.10129441/)
- van der Vennet & Serice (2012), *Art Therapy* 29(2): replication, 50 graduate/undergraduate students; mandala colouring reduced state anxiety (STAI) significantly more than plaid or blank-page colouring (so the plaid = mandala result of the original did not replicate; the mandala benefit did). [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2012.680047](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.680047)
- Mantzios & Giannou (2018), *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:56: Exp. 1 found **no difference** between unguided mandala colouring and free drawing; Exp. 2 (n = 72 students) found mindfulness-guided colouring did better for anxiety than unguided colouring, with no change in mindfulness. [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00056](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00056/epub)
- Holt, Furbert & Sweetingham (2019), *Art Therapy* 36(4):200–208: two randomised controlled crossover studies with an active control (reading); colouring significantly reduced anxiety and increased mindfulness (and hedonic tone) vs control and baseline. Colouring duration (I recall 20 min) **[unverified]**. — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2019.1645498](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/848019/cognitive-and-affective-benefits-of-coloring-two-randomized-controlled-crossover-studies)
- Flett et al. (2017), *Creativity Research Journal*, "Sharpen your pencils: Preliminary evidence that adult coloring reduces depressive symptoms and anxiety": daily colouring associated with lower depressive symptoms and anxiety. One-week daily design, sample size and puzzle-control details are from memory **[unverified]**. — [cited in review](https://czasopisma.bg.ug.edu.pl/index.php/HPR/article/download/7948/7043/11759)
- Eaton & Tieber (2017), *Art Therapy* 34(1):42–46: free-choice colouring improved anxiety and perseverance relative to forced-choice colouring. [secondary-source verified] — [JISS review](https://www.jiss.org/documents/volume_8/JISS%202018%208%281%29%201-21%20Coloring%20and%20Mindfulness.pdf)
- An RCT in older Taiwanese adults using the Curry & Kasser designs found significantly lower anxiety only in the mandala-colouring group vs control. — [PMC6996682](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6996682)

#### Clay / sculpting
- Kimport & Robbins (2012), *Art Therapy* 29(2):74–79: RCT, 102 adults, negative mood induction, then clay (structured pinch-pot or free manipulation) vs soft stress ball (structured or free); greater mood enhancement after clay than ball handling. Minutes of clay work not in abstract. [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2012.680048](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.680048)
- Toyoshima, Fukui & Kuda (2011), *International Journal of Music Education* 29:257–264: 57 college students, 30-min sessions of piano, clay moulding, calligraphy or silent rest; cortisol fell after all three creative activities and state anxiety fell in all active groups but not control; piano's cortisol drop exceeded clay's. — [Berklee citation record](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/445); [summary](https://humanstress.ca/piano-playing-reduces-stress)

#### Painting, collage
- No experiment isolating painting or collage alone in non-clinical adults was located; both appear only as options within mixed-media sessions (Sandmire 2012; Kaimal 2016, above). — [Sandmire DOI](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.683748); [Kaimal PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5004743)

#### Crafts / knitting / crochet
- Riley, Corkhill & Morris (2013), *British Journal of Occupational Therapy* 76(2):50–57: international online survey of over 3,500 knitters (the paper's N is usually cited as 3,545; respondents ~98.8% female per a later write-up); knitting reported for relaxation and stress relief; knitting in a group was significantly associated with perceived happiness and improved social contact. The specific association "more frequent knitting ↔ feeling calm and happy" is from memory of the abstract **[unverified]**. Cross-sectional, self-selected, self-report: cannot show causation. — [DOI 10.4276/030802213X13603244419077](https://stitchlinks.com/research1.html)

#### Expressive writing (Pennebaker paradigm)
- Frattaroli (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(6):823–865: random-effects meta-analysis of 146 randomised experimental-disclosure studies; average effect r = .075 (≈ d 0.15), positive and significant but small. [verified] — [full text PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf)
- Smyth (1998): earlier meta-analysis of 13 studies in healthy samples, overall d = 0.47 — an estimate that later, larger meta-analyses did not sustain. — [Smyth 1998 PDF](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/smyth_1998_-_written_emotional_expression.pdf)
- Reinhold, Bürkner & Holling (2018), *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice*: 39 RCTs (64 comparisons) in physically healthy adults; expressive writing produced **no significant long-term effect on depressive symptoms**; effects larger with more sessions and more specific topics. [abstract-verified] — [author PDF](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf)
- Guo (2023), *British Journal of Clinical Psychology*: 31 RCTs, N = 4,012, healthy/subclinical; Hedges' g = −0.12 (95% CI −0.21 to −0.04) on depression, anxiety and stress, with the effect **emerging only at delayed follow-up**. [verified via secondary summary; DOI not confirmed] — [summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)
- Baikie & Wilhelm (2005), *Advances in Psychiatric Treatment*: "The immediate impact of expressive writing is usually a short-term increase in distress, negative mood and physical symptoms, and a decrease in positive mood compared with controls"; emotional-health benefits less robust than physical ones. Standard dose 15–20 min on 3–5 occasions. — [DOI 10.1192/apt.11.5.338](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/baikie_wilhelm_2005_-_emotional_and_physical_health_benefits_of_expressive_writing.pdf)

#### Journaling (general) and positive-affect journaling
- Sohal, Singh, Dhillon & Gill (2022), *Family Medicine and Community Health*: systematic review/meta-analysis of 20 RCTs of journaling for mental illness symptoms; described as a small-to-moderate benefit; longer interventions (>30 days) better for depression. I recall the pooled result as ~5% greater symptom-score reduction vs control (anxiety ~9%, PTSD ~6%, depression ~2%) but could not confirm these figures **[unverified]**; high heterogeneity, clinical/symptomatic samples. — [PubMed 35304431](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35304431/)
- Smyth et al. (2018), *JMIR Mental Health*: preliminary RCT of online positive affect journaling in general medical patients with elevated anxiety; reduced mental distress/anxiety. Dose (15 min, 3 days/week, 12 weeks; N ≈ 70) from memory **[unverified]**. — [DOI 10.2196/11290](https://mental.jmir.org/2018/4/e11290/)

#### "Best possible self" (BPS) writing
- Carrillo et al. (2019), *PLOS ONE* 14(9):e0222386: meta-analysis, 29 studies, 2,909 participants; vs controls BPS improved positive affect d+ = .511, optimism d+ = .334, wellbeing d+ = .325; **small** effects on negative affect and depressive symptoms; vs gratitude interventions BPS was better on positive affect (d+ = .326) and negative affect (d+ = .485); trend for more total minutes of practice → larger effect. Typical single dose in the primary studies is ~15–20 min writing/imagery **[typical dose from memory]**. [abstract-verified] — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0222386](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6756746/)

#### Poetry (reading/writing)
- A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of poetry-based interventions in *Psychiatry Research* (cited as Kassab et al.; 15 studies) reports moderate-to-large effects on PTSD, depression, anxiety and stress but flags small samples, variable quality and possible publication bias. Seen only via secondary summaries. **[unverified]** — [APA blog](https://www.psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/healing-power-of-poetry)
- A small 2025 Spanish-language meta-analysis (7 studies, 377 participants) reported g = −1.12 (95% CI −1.72 to −0.53) with high heterogeneity — unstable estimate. — [Revista de Psicoterapia](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/08_psicoterapia_v36n131_2025.pdf)

#### Music listening (relaxing, self-selected, sad)
- de Witte, Spruit, van Hooren, Moonen & Stams (2020), *Health Psychology Review* 14(2):294–324: two multilevel meta-analyses, 104 RCTs, 327 effect sizes, 9,617 participants; music interventions reduced stress: physiological d = .380, psychological d = .545; heart rate d = .456, blood pressure d = .343, hormones (incl. cortisol) d = .349. Many trials are in medical settings. [abstract-verified] — [PubMed 31167611](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31167611/) (DOI 10.1080/17437199.2019.1627897)
- de Witte et al. (2022, online 2020), *Health Psychology Review*: separate meta-analysis of therapist-delivered **music therapy** for stress, overall d = .723 — not applicable to solo self-guided listening; do not conflate with the .38/.545 figures. — [PubMed 33176590](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33176590/)
- Harney, Johnson, Bailes & Havelka (2023), *Musicae Scientiae* 27(2):278–298: 24 controlled studies (21 pooled); music listening reduced state anxiety, d = −0.77 (large). — [open-access record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/182421/)
- Panteleeva, Ceschi, Glowinski, Courvoisier & Grandjean (2018), *Psychology of Music* 46(4):473–487: meta-analysis in **non-clinical** samples; self-reported anxiety d = −0.30 (95% CI −0.55 to −0.04); pooled psychophysiological effect not significant. — [UNIGE archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:109589)
- Linnemann, Ditzen, Strahler, Doerr & Nater (2015), *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 60:82–90: ecological momentary assessment in daily life (cortisol subsample n = 25); mere music listening reduced subjective stress (p = .010); strongest when the stated reason was **relaxation** (lower subjective stress and lower cortisol); listening to reduce boredom or for distraction was not associated with stress reduction. — [PubMed 26142566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26142566/)
- Taruffi & Koelsch (2014), *PLOS ONE*: survey of 772 listeners; sad music most often evokes nostalgia rather than sadness and is reported to provide consolation and regulation of negative mood; appreciation higher in people with high empathy and low emotional stability. Self-report survey. — [PMC4203803](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203803)
- Garrido & Schubert (2015), *Music Perception*: 175 university students listened to self-selected sad music; depression scores significantly **increased** in those with high rumination, who nevertheless liked the music. — [record](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/467)

#### Singing / playing an instrument
- Fancourt, Aufegger & Williamon (2015), *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:1242: 15 professional singers; cortisol and cortisone fell significantly during low-stress singing (rehearsal, no audience) and **rose** during high-stress live performance. Tiny sample of professionals. — [DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01242](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4559645)
- Toyoshima et al. (2011): 30 min piano playing lowered cortisol and state anxiety, more than clay or calligraphy for cortisol (57 students across 4 groups, so ~14 per group; participants could play). — [record](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/445)

#### Dancing
- Koch et al. (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:1806: meta-analysis, 41 controlled studies (N = 2,374; 21 dance movement therapy, 20 dance); overall medium effect d = 0.60, I² = 72.6%; outcome clusters d = 0.53–0.85; dance-only cluster d = 0.81 (I² = 78%), DMT cluster d = 0.30; DMT decreased depression and anxiety and increased quality of life; larger effects on observer-rated measures (possible bias). Mostly multi-week group programmes. — [DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01806](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31481910/)
- Campion & Levita (2014), *Journal of Positive Psychology* 9(2):137–145: young non-clinical adults; a single short bout of dancing (I recall 5 min — **[unverified]**) and passive music listening each raised positive affect, lowered negative affect and **reduced fatigue**; cycling and sitting quietly did not change positive mood or fatigue. — [record](https://www.instituteofcoaching.org/resources/enhancing-positive-affect-and-divergent-thinking-abilities-play-some-music-and-dance)

#### Cooking / baking
- Farmer, Touchton-Leonard & Ross (2018), *Health Education & Behavior* 45(2):167–180: systematic review, 11 studies included of 377 retrieved; group cooking interventions showed positive influences on socialisation, self-esteem/confidence (2 studies), quality of life (2 studies) and mood/affect (3 studies); authors call the evidence preliminary and limited. Interventions were group classes/community kitchens, often clinical or rehabilitation settings — not solo home cooking. — [PubMed 29121776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29121776/) (DOI 10.1177/1090198117736352)
- Cooking/baking was popularised as mood-boosting by press coverage of Conner et al. (2018), but that study measured overall daily creative activity, not cooking specifically. — [Smithsonian coverage](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/feeling-down-scientists-say-cooking-and-baking-may-help-you-feel-better-180961223/)

#### Photography
- Chen, Mark & Ali (2016), *Psychology of Well-Being* 6:8: 4-week study, 41 college students, one photo per day in one of three conditions (smiling selfie; photo of something that makes you happy; photo to make someone else happy); positive affect increased in all conditions after 3 weeks; the "make others happy" group became calmer and reported reduced stress through connection with friends/family. No no-photo control group. — [DOI 10.1186/s13612-016-0044-4](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4932122/)

#### Everyday creativity (general)
- Conner, DeYoung & Silvia (2018; online 2016), *Journal of Positive Psychology* 13(2):181–189: 13-day daily diary, n = 658 young adults (University of Otago); people reported higher activated positive affect and flourishing on days **after** they were more creative than usual; prior-day positive affect did not predict next-day creativity; no reported effect on negative affect [last point from memory of abstract — **unverified**]. Observational within-person lagged design; student sample. — [DOI 10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049)

#### Reading fiction / bibliotherapy
- Carney & Robertson (2022), *PLOS ONE* 17(4):e0266323: five studies; fiction improved mood/emotion measures only after memory/cognitive consolidation (recalling, discussing); **"simply being exposed to fiction does not produce an immediate effect on well-being."** — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0266323](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35395034/)
- Gualano et al. (2017), *Clinical Psychology Review*: systematic review of 8 RCTs (1,347 participants) of bibliotherapy for depression; adults' depressive symptoms reduced at 3-month to 3-year follow-up; no significant effect in young people. Note: this is mostly structured **self-help (CBT-based) books**, not fiction. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cpr.2017.09.006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28993103/)

### Inferences
- For an app delivering 5–30 min solo activities, the best-supported pairings are: stress/anxiety → relaxing or self-selected music; anxiety → structured colouring; sadness/anger (acute) → drawing something neutral or positive; low positive affect → best-possible-self writing; fatigue → brief dancing or upbeat music (single study).
- Therapist-led art/music/dance therapy effect sizes (Joschko SMD 0.38; de Witte d = .72; Koch) should not be transferred to self-guided versions; they are an upper bound from a different delivery mode and mostly clinical samples.
- Evidence strength ordering: music listening > BPS writing > colouring ≈ drawing-to-distract > expressive writing (reliable but tiny and delayed) > clay / mixed art-making > dance (solo, brief) > knitting, cooking, photography, poetry, fiction, everyday creativity.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence located that isolates painting, collage, crochet, baking or solo photography as single-session mood interventions in non-clinical adults.
- No direct evidence for **loneliness, boredom or overwhelm** as outcomes of solo creative activity. The closest findings are social (group knitting, community cooking, pro-social photos) and therefore not solo; and Linnemann 2015 found music used *to relieve boredom* did not reduce stress.
- Standardised effect sizes for the single-session drawing, colouring and clay experiments were not available from abstracts.
- The often-cited "6 minutes of reading reduces stress by 68%" claim is not from a peer-reviewed paper as far as I know and was not searched or verified here; it is excluded.

## Q2. What dose was tested? Is a single short session effective?

### Takeaway
Yes for immediate state change: single sessions of roughly 5–45 minutes reduce state anxiety, negative mood or cortisol for art-making, colouring, clay, piano, dancing and music listening. No for expressive writing: its tested dose is multiple sessions and benefits appear weeks later, while the immediate effect is a mood dip.

### Cited Findings
- 45 min free art-making → lower cortisol (one-group pre/post). — [Kaimal 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5004743)
- 30 min piano / clay / calligraphy → lower cortisol and state anxiety vs silent rest. — [Toyoshima 2011](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/445)
- Single brief art-making session before exams → lower state anxiety vs control. — [Sandmire 2012](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.683748)
- Single-session colouring reduces anxiety in randomised crossover and between-group designs. — [Holt 2019](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/848019/cognitive-and-affective-benefits-of-coloring-two-randomized-controlled-crossover-studies); [van der Vennet & Serice 2012](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.680047)
- Single-session drawing after mood induction gives short-term mood repair (the papers' own titles say "short-term"). — [Dalebroux 2008](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1); [Drake, Coleman & Winner 2011](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/07421656.2011.557032)
- Short-duration dancing or music listening improved affect and fatigue in one session. — [Campion & Levita 2014](https://www.instituteofcoaching.org/resources/enhancing-positive-affect-and-divergent-thinking-abilities-play-some-music-and-dance)
- Expressive writing: standard protocol is 15–20 min on 3–5 occasions; more sessions and more specific topics → larger effects; overall effect emerges at delayed follow-up, not immediately. — [Baikie & Wilhelm 2005](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/baikie_wilhelm_2005_-_emotional_and_physical_health_benefits_of_expressive_writing.pdf); [Reinhold 2018](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf); [Guo 2023 summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)
- BPS writing: trend for larger effects with more total minutes of practice. — [Carrillo 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6756746/)
- Journaling: interventions longer than 30 days were more effective for depression. — [Sohal 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35304431/)
- Everyday creativity: benefit detected the **next day**, not necessarily the same day. — [Conner 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049)
- Fiction: no immediate wellbeing effect from a single exposure. — [Carney & Robertson 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35395034/)

### Inferences
- The app can honestly describe drawing, colouring, clay, music and dancing as "can help right now"; expressive writing and journaling should be framed as "pays off over days/weeks, may feel heavier at first"; everyday creativity as "tends to lift tomorrow's mood".
- Nearly all single-session evidence measures mood minutes after the activity; durability beyond the session is essentially untested.

### Gaps
- No dose-response trials comparing 5 vs 10 vs 20 vs 30 min for any visual-art activity were found. The shortest verified visual-art dose in this note is "brief"/~20–30 min; a 5-min version is an extrapolation.
- Exact minutes for Curry & Kasser, Holt, Kimport & Robbins, Drake & Winner, Sandmire and Campion & Levita could not be confirmed from abstracts.

## Q3. Verification status of the specifically requested studies

### Takeaway
All named studies exist and their headline findings match how they are usually cited, with three corrections: (a) Kaimal 2016 had no control group and the "75%" figure is press-release-level; (b) van der Vennet & Serice replicated the mandala benefit but not the plaid = mandala equivalence; (c) "de Witte 2020" (music interventions, d = .38/.545) and "de Witte 2022" (music therapy, d = .723) are different papers.

### Cited Findings
- Kaimal et al. 2016 — exists; 39 adults, 45 min, cortisol lowered, no moderation by experience; uncontrolled. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5004743)
- Curry & Kasser 2005 — exists; 84 undergraduates; replication recorded as successful. — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/07421656.2005.10129441/)
- van der Vennet & Serice 2012 — exists; n = 50; mandala > plaid and blank. — [journal](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421656.2012.680047)
- Drake & Winner 2012; Dalebroux et al. 2008 — exist; distraction/positive drawing > venting. — [Drake & Winner](https://typeset.io/pdf/confronting-sadness-through-art-making-distraction-is-more-4yr4tzvok3.pdf); [Dalebroux](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1)
- Frattaroli 2006 — exists; 146 studies, r = .075. — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf)
- Null/negative expressive-writing findings — Reinhold 2018 (no long-term effect on depressive symptoms); Baikie & Wilhelm 2005 (immediate distress increase). — [Reinhold](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf); [Baikie & Wilhelm](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/baikie_wilhelm_2005_-_emotional_and_physical_health_benefits_of_expressive_writing.pdf)
- de Witte 2020 and 2022 — exist as two separate meta-analyses. — [2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31167611/); [2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33176590/)
- Conner, DeYoung & Silvia 2018 — exists; n = 658, 13 days. — [journal](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049)
- Fancourt & Finn 2019 — exists; WHO HEN report 67. — [record](https://www.jameelartshealthlab.org/research/research-projects/2019-who-report)
- Riley et al. 2013 — exists; citation and "over 3,500 respondents" confirmed; detailed percentages not confirmed. — [Stitchlinks research page](https://stitchlinks.com/research1.html)
- Farmer et al. 2018 — exists; 11 studies. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29121776/)
- Recent art-therapy meta-analyses — Joschko 2024 (JAMA Netw Open) confirmed; a 2026 depression meta-analysis seen in summary only. — [Joschko](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393726)

### Inferences
- Popular summaries overstate Kaimal 2016 ("art lowers cortisol in 75% of people") relative to what an uncontrolled n = 39 study can show; cortisol also falls with time of day and rest.

### Gaps
- Full texts were not accessible, so in-paper statistics (means, t/F values, CIs) were not extracted for the single-session experiments.

## Q4. Contraindications and backfire cases

### Takeaway
Three backfire patterns are documented: expressive/trauma writing makes people feel worse immediately; venting-style art (and venting generally) does not repair mood and is worse than distraction; and sad music worsens mood in people prone to rumination. Performance pressure also reverses the stress benefit of singing.

### Cited Findings
- Expressive writing typically produces a short-term increase in distress and negative mood and a decrease in positive mood right after writing. — [Baikie & Wilhelm 2005](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/baikie_wilhelm_2005_-_emotional_and_physical_health_benefits_of_expressive_writing.pdf)
- Expressive writing has no significant long-term effect on depressive symptoms in healthy adults. — [Reinhold 2018](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf)
- Drawing to vent was no better than a non-art control task for mood repair, and worse than positive or distracting drawing, for both sadness and anger. — [Dalebroux 2008](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1); [Genuth & Drake 2021](https://www.ovid.com/journals/paca/pdf/10.1037/aca0000265~the-benefits-of-drawing-to-regulate-sadness-and-anger)
- Writing was less effective than drawing for short-term mood repair after a sad induction. — [Drake, Coleman & Winner 2011](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/07421656.2011.557032)
- For anger specifically, a 2024 meta-analytic review (154 studies, 10,189 participants; Kjærvik & Bushman, *Clinical Psychology Review*) found arousal-decreasing activities reduce anger while venting and arousal-increasing activities do not. Seen via press coverage only; effect sizes **[unverified]**. — [coverage](https://www.sciencealert.com/venting-doesnt-reduce-anger-but-something-else-does-research-finds)
- Self-selected sad music increased depressed mood in high ruminators. — [Garrido & Schubert 2015](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/467)
- Singing under performance/evaluation pressure raised cortisol; low-stress singing lowered it. — [Fancourt et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4559645)
- Unstructured (blank-page) colouring was less anxiolytic than structured designs, and unguided mandala colouring was not better than free drawing in one experiment. — [Curry & Kasser 2005](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/07421656.2005.10129441/); [Mantzios & Giannou 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00056/epub)
- Music listened to for boredom relief or distraction was not associated with stress reduction in daily life. — [Linnemann 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26142566/)

### Inferences
- When the detected state is acute sadness, anger or anxiety, the app should default to distraction-type or positive-content prompts ("draw something you can see", "draw something that makes you happy", structured colouring, calming music) and avoid "draw/write how you feel" as the immediate suggestion.
- Expressive writing should not be offered as a quick fix for acute distress; if offered, warn that feeling worse right afterwards is normal, and do not offer it to someone who appears highly distressed with no follow-up.
- For anger, prefer low-arousal creative options (colouring, clay, calm music) over high-energy venting ones (aggressive scribbling, loud angry music); this is an inference from Kjærvik & Bushman plus the drawing studies, not a directly tested arts finding.
- For low mood with rumination, steer away from sad music; the consolation benefits in Taruffi & Koelsch are survey self-reports from general listeners.
- Any singing/instrument/dance suggestion should be framed as private and non-evaluative.

### Gaps
- No study was found testing whether colouring/drawing/knitting worsens mood in perfectionistic or low-skill users, or whether creative activities increase frustration when users feel overwhelmed.

## Q5. Does skill level or prior interest moderate the benefit?

### Takeaway
The limited evidence suggests art-making benefits do not require skill, but preference/choice and using music for the right reason matter. Evidence is thin and mostly null-moderation findings from underpowered studies.

### Cited Findings
- No significant differences in cortisol reduction by prior art-making experience, media choice or gender (n = 39; underpowered for moderation). — [Kaimal 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5004743)
- Free-choice colouring improved anxiety and perseverance more than forced-choice colouring. — [Eaton & Tieber 2017, via review](https://www.jiss.org/documents/volume_8/JISS%202018%208%281%29%201-21%20Coloring%20and%20Mindfulness.pdf)
- Drake & Hodge (2015), *Art Therapy* 32(1):27–33, tested whether preference for drawing vs writing matters for short-term affect regulation; I recall drawing improving mood more than writing regardless of stated preference, but I could only confirm the citation, not the result **[unverified]**. — [DOI 10.1080/07421656.2015.995032, cited in](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/07421656.2011.557032)
- Appreciation of sad music is higher in people with high empathy and low emotional stability; effect on mood depends on rumination tendency. — [Taruffi & Koelsch 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203803); [Garrido & Schubert 2015](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/467)
- Music's stress-reducing effect in daily life depends on the listener's reason (relaxation works best). — [Linnemann 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26142566/)
- Instrument and singing evidence comes from people who could already play/sing (college students at piano; professional singers). — [Toyoshima 2011](https://remix.berklee.edu/mhi-music-mental-health/445); [Fancourt 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4559645)
- Knitting evidence comes entirely from self-selected existing knitters. — [Riley 2013](https://stitchlinks.com/research1.html)

### Inferences
- Low-skill-barrier activities (colouring, simple drawing, clay, music listening) are safe defaults for any user; instrument playing and knitting should be suggested only to users who already have the skill/interest (use preference tags).
- Giving the user a choice among 2–3 creative options is itself supported (free-choice colouring; self-selected music).

### Gaps
- No adequately powered moderation analysis by artistic skill, trait creativity or prior interest was found for any activity.
- Whether beginners find instrument playing or knitting stressful rather than calming is untested in the sources found.
