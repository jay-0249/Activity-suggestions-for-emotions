## 1. LONELINESS: what works, which brief self-initiated actions have evidence, and does passive social media worsen it?

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses show loneliness interventions work with small-to-moderate effects (g about 0.2–0.5), and the best-supported ingredient is changing maladaptive social cognition, not merely adding contact. For a 5–30 minute "right now" action, the best experimental evidence is for reaching out by voice, brief weak-tie or stranger conversations, nostalgia, and sharing good news; these are single-session experiments on mood and connection, not trials of chronic loneliness.

### Cited Findings
- Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo (2011), Personality and Social Psychology Review 15(3): single-group pre-post and non-randomized studies gave larger effects than randomized studies; among randomized studies the most successful interventions addressed maladaptive social cognition; pooled RCT effect was "small but significant" (20 RCTs). [V] — [DOI 10.1177/1088868310377394](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394); [UChicago summary](https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/2010/september/addressing-negative-thoughts-most-effective-in-fighting-loneliness)
- Exact Masi values as I recall them (pre-post −0.37, non-randomized −0.46, randomized −0.198; social-cognition RCT subgroup −0.598): **[U] unverified**; one low-quality blog echoed −0.598, no primary confirmation obtained. — [open copy PMC3865701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3865701)
- Hickin et al. (2021), Clinical Psychology Review: 28 studies, N = 3,039; psychological interventions reduced loneliness vs control, g = 0.43; considerable heterogeneity; intervention type approached significance as moderator. [V] — [DOI 10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102066](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102066); [UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10135088/)
- Zagic et al. (2022), Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology 57: RCTs in adults; effect on objective social contact g = 0.43 and on perceived quality of social connection g = −0.33 (note: different outcomes from Hickin's loneliness g). [V] — [DOI 10.1007/s00127-021-02191-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-021-02191-w)
- Lasgaard et al. (2022), European Journal of Public Health (conference abstract; 128 studies): pooled SMD = −0.47 for 54 RCTs (n = 6,379), −0.24 for multi-cohort, −0.42 for single-cohort studies; unclear who benefits most. [V] — [PMC9593938](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9593938/)
- Full version, Lasgaard et al. (2025), American Psychologist: 280 studies; small-to-moderate short-term effect; effects at 1–6 months comparable; psychological interventions most effective (moderate); certainty of evidence "low to very low". [V, abstract/press level] — [DOI 10.1037/amp0001578](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001578); [APA release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/10/loneliness-interventions)
- Verduyn et al. (2015), JEP: General 144(2): lab experiment plus experience sampling; passive Facebook use caused/predicted declines in affective well-being over time, mediated by envy; active use did not. Outcome was affective well-being, not loneliness per se. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000057](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000057); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25706656/)
- Hunt et al. (2018), J. Social and Clinical Psychology: limiting social media to about 30 min/day for 3 weeks reduced loneliness and depression in undergraduates (N = 143). [U] — [DOI 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751)
- Kumar & Epley (2021), JEP: General: voice-based contact (phone, video, voice chat) created stronger social bonds than text with no increase in awkwardness; people mispredicted this and so preferred text. [V, secondary sources; year listed as 2020 online / 2021 print] — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000962](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000962) (DOI [U])
- Liu, Rim, Min & Min (2023), JPSP 124(4), 754–771: initiators underestimate how much recipients appreciate being contacted; gap is larger for surprising reach-outs (e.g., weak ties). [V] — [DOI 10.1037/pspi0000402](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402) (DOI [U])
- Epley & Schroeder (2014), JEP: General: Chicago train/bus commuters instructed to talk to a stranger had the most positive commute, solitude the least; a separate sample predicted the opposite. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/a0037323](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323); [author PDF](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/jschroeder/Publications/Epley%26Schroeder2014.pdf)
- Replication: Schroeder, Lyons & Epley (2022), JEP: General, "Hello, stranger?" replicated the pattern with London commuters. [U] — [DOI 10.1037/xge0001118](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001118)
- Sandstrom & Dunn (2014), PSPB 40(7), 910–922: people reported more happiness and belonging on days with more weak-tie interactions than usual (within-person, observational). [V] — [DOI 10.1177/0146167214529799](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214529799)
- Sandstrom & Dunn (2014), Social Psychological and Personality Science 5(4), 437–442: customers randomly assigned to have a genuine brief social exchange with a barista (smile, eye contact, short chat) reported more positive affect (and belonging) than those told to be efficient. [V] — [DOI 10.1177/1948550613502990](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550613502990)
- Zhou, Sedikides, Wildschut & Gao (2008), Psychological Science: loneliness reduces perceived social support but also triggers nostalgia, which in turn raises perceived social support. [V, via secondary description] — [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02194.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02194.x)
- Lambert et al. (2013), JSPR 30(1), 24–43: sharing positive experiences boosts positive affect; later review summarises link with less loneliness and more happiness/life satisfaction. [V, secondary] — [DOI 10.1177/0265407512449400](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407512449400)
- Kahlon et al. (2021), JAMA Psychiatry: RCT of 4 weeks of empathy-focused phone calls from lay callers reduced loneliness, depression and anxiety in homebound adults (N = 240). Receiving, not initiating, calls. [U] — [DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.0113](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.0113)
- Pet/animal contact: Pendry & Vandagriff (2019), AERA Open: RCT, 249 undergraduates; 10 minutes of hands-on petting of cats/dogs produced lower post-test salivary cortisol than watching, slideshow or waitlist. Stress outcome, not loneliness. [V] — [DOI 10.1177/2332858419852592](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419852592)
- Caution for socially anxious users: Alden & Trew (2013), Emotion: in socially anxious individuals, 4 weeks of kind acts increased positive affect (kind acts as a lower-threat route to connection). [U] — [DOI 10.1037/a0027761](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027761)
- Caution on "act extraverted" prompts: Jacques-Hamilton, Sun & Smillie (2019), JEP: General: acting extraverted for a week raised positive affect on average, but introverts gained less and reported costs (fatigue, inauthenticity). [U] — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000516](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000516)

### Inferences
- App ranking for loneliness: (1) voice/video call a friend or family member; (2) send an unexpected "thinking of you" message; (3) brief chat with a barista, neighbour or acquaintance; (4) small kind act; (5) 5-minute nostalgia or photo reminiscence when no one is reachable; (6) pet contact. Add a cognitive reframe line ("they will appreciate it more than you expect"), because meta-analyses point to social cognition as the active ingredient and the Epley/Liu work shows the barrier is misprediction.
- Suggest replacing passive scrolling with an active message to one person; the evidence concerns affect and envy, so phrase as "may leave you feeling worse", not "causes loneliness".
- For socially anxious users, offer graded options (text before call; kind act; nostalgia; pet) and never only stranger-talk.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis isolates brief (under 30 min), self-initiated, single-dose actions for loneliness; meta-analytic effects come from multi-week programmes.
- Zagic et al. loneliness-specific pooled effect not confirmed. Masi exact effect sizes unverified.
- Pet interaction evidence is for stress/cortisol and mood in students; evidence for loneliness specifically was not verified.
- No verified RCT found in this session of "acts of kindness reduce loneliness" in general adults.

## 2. BOREDOM: what it signals and what relieves it

### Takeaway
Boredom is a functional signal that the current activity is not engaging attention (too easy or too hard) and/or lacks meaning; the remedy is not just stimulation but an activity that is challenge-skill matched and personally meaningful or novel. Experimental support exists for flow-calibrated games and for nostalgia restoring meaning; much of the rest is theory.

### Cited Findings
- Westgate & Wilson (2018), Psychological Review 125(5), 689–713 (MAC model): boredom arises independently from an attention deficit (mismatch between cognitive demands and resources, i.e., understimulation OR overstimulation) and a meaning deficit (activity incongruent with valued goals). Boredom can motivate interesting rather than merely enjoyable activities. [V, abstract] — [DOI 10.1037/rev0000097](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000097); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963873/)
- Eastwood, Frischen, Fenske & Smilek (2012), Perspectives on Psychological Science 7(5): boredom defined as "the aversive experience of wanting, but being unable, to engage in satisfying activity", rooted in attention failure. [U] — [DOI 10.1177/1745691612456044](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612456044)
- van Tilburg & Igou (2012), Motivation and Emotion: boredom is distinguished from other negative emotions by perceived lack of challenge and lack of meaning; it prompts meaning-seeking. [U] — [DOI 10.1007/s11031-011-9234-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-011-9234-9)
- van Tilburg, Igou & Sedikides (2013), Emotion 13(3), 450–461: six studies; induced boredom increased nostalgia; search for meaning mediated the effect; nostalgia counteracted the meaninglessness of boredom. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/a0030442](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030442); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23163710/)
- Bench & Lench (2013), Behavioral Sciences 3(3): functional account; boredom signals that the current goal is no longer rewarding and motivates pursuit of new goals/experiences. [U] — [DOI 10.3390/bs3030459](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs3030459)
- Wilson et al. (2014), Science: many participants disliked 6–15 minutes alone with their thoughts; some chose mild electric shock over doing nothing (shows unguided "just sit" is aversive for many; and that boredom can drive poor choices). [U] — [DOI 10.1126/science.1250830](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1250830)
- Rankin, Walsh & Sweeny (2019), Emotion 19, 818–828: Study 3 randomized 309 undergraduates to Tetris that was too easy (boring), adaptive (flow), or too hard; the adaptive condition produced more flow, less negative and more positive emotion. Two observational studies: more flow during stressful waiting periods related to less worry. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/emo0000479](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000479); [UCR summary](https://news.ucr.edu/articles/2018/10/26/tetris-it-could-be-salve-worried-mind)
- Board games: Noda, Shirotsuki & Nakao (2019), BioPsychoSocial Medicine, systematic review: board games studied mostly for cognition/education and clinical symptoms; study quality limited; little direct evidence on boredom or momentary mood. [U] — [DOI 10.1186/s13030-019-0164-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13030-019-0164-1)
- Jigsaw puzzles: Fissler et al. (2018), Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience: RCT in adults 50+; outcomes were visuospatial cognition, not mood or boredom. [U] — [DOI 10.3389/fnagi.2018.00299](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnagi.2018.00299)

### Inferences
- Diagnose the type of boredom in the app if possible: "understimulated" → raise challenge (adaptive puzzle/game, learn a micro-skill, timed challenge); "meaningless" → meaningful action (kind act, message someone, nostalgia, progress on a valued goal); "overstimulated/too hard" → simplify or break the task down.
- Flow-matched games, learning something new, and creative tasks fit the MAC model, but only the adaptive-game finding is directly experimental; "learn something new for boredom" is theory-derived.
- Avoid suggesting passive scrolling or "sit quietly" as boredom fixes.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of boredom-relief interventions in general adults was found.
- No verified experimental evidence that puzzles or board games reduce state boredom; evidence is indirect (flow theory).
- Danckert's work (boredom as a self-regulatory signal) was not opened this session; no specific Danckert citation is asserted.

## 3. FATIGUE / low energy: microbreaks, naps, caffeine nap, short walks, daylight, hydration, music

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks (10 min or less) reliably raise vigor and reduce fatigue with small effects (d about 0.35), and a 10–20 minute nap gives a small alertness gain for about two hours; both are meta-analytically supported. Sleepiness (sleep pressure) is best met with a nap or caffeine+nap, whereas mental fatigue from sustained effort responds to breaks, movement and nature views; hydration and music have weaker or indirect evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Albulescu et al. (2022), PLoS ONE 17(8): e0272460: 22 samples, N = 2,335; micro-breaks (≤10 min) increased vigor d = .36 (k = 9, n = 913) and reduced fatigue d = .35 (k = 9, n = 803); performance overall non-significant d = .16 (k = 15), significant only for less cognitively demanding tasks; longer breaks → larger performance gain; highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 minutes. [V] — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0272460](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- Dutheil et al. (2021), IJERPH 18(19): 10212: 11 lab studies, 381 participants; post-nap cognitive performance effect size 0.18 (95% CI 0.09–0.27); alertness 0.29 (0.10–0.48); benefits mainly up to 120 min after waking; mixed results during sleep inertia; early-afternoon naps (before 1 pm) trend larger but CI crosses zero. [V] — [DOI 10.3390/ijerph181910212](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph181910212); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34639511/)
- Brooks & Lack (2006), Sleep 29(6): after restricted night sleep, a 10-minute nap gave immediate improvement in sleepiness, fatigue, vigor and cognition; a 5-minute nap gave little benefit; longer naps (20–30 min) had sleep inertia before benefits. [V for 5/10-min, U for longer naps] — [DOI 10.1093/sleep/29.6.831](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831)
- Reyner & Horne (1997), Psychophysiology: 12 sleepy drivers; caffeine plus a <15-min nap in a 30-min break before a 2-h monotonous simulator drive; combination reduced incidents to about 9% of placebo vs 34% for caffeine alone (secondary summary). Very small sample. [V] — [DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8986.1997.tb02148.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1997.tb02148.x); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9401427/)
- Hayashi, Masuda & Hori (2003), Clinical Neurophysiology 114(12), 2268–2278: tested caffeine, bright light and face washing after a short nap; citation confirmed; results not retrieved (my recollection that caffeine+nap was most effective is **unverified**). — [DOI 10.1016/S1388-2457(03)00255-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1388-2457(03)00255-4)
- Thayer (1987), JPSP 52(1): a 10-minute brisk walk increased self-rated energy and reduced tension for up to about 2 hours, whereas a sugar snack increased tension and later tiredness. [U] — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.52.1.119](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.1.119)
- Ohly et al. (2016), J. Toxicology and Environmental Health Part B (systematic review of attention restoration theory): 31 studies; meta-analyses supported nature exposure on only 3 attention measures; 10 others showed no clear benefit; small samples and inconsistent outcomes. [V] — [DOI 10.1080/10937404.2016.1196155](https://doi.org/10.1080/10937404.2016.1196155)
- Lee et al. (2015), J. Environmental Psychology: 150 students; a 40-second view of a flowering green roof (vs concrete roof) mid-task led to fewer omission errors and more consistent responding on a sustained attention task. Single experiment. [V] — [DOI 10.1016/j.jenvp.2015.04.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2015.04.003)
- Daylight/bright light: Smolders, de Kort & Cluitmans (2012), Physiology & Behavior: 1 h of 1000 lux vs 200 lux at eye level during daytime increased subjective alertness and vitality. [U] — [DOI 10.1016/j.physbeh.2012.04.028](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2012.04.028)
- Hydration: Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford (2018), Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise: 33 studies, 413 subjects; dehydration impaired cognition ES = −0.21 (95% CI −0.31 to −0.11); larger at >2% body mass loss (−0.28) than ≤2% (−0.14, CI to 0.00); attention/executive tasks most affected. This shows dehydration harms, not that drinking water boosts energy in hydrated people. [V] — [DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001682](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001682)
- Music for energy: Thayer, Newman & McClain (1994), JPSP 67(5): self-report studies in which people named listening to music among effective self-regulation strategies for changing bad mood/raising energy. Self-report, not an experiment. [U] — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910)
- Recovery experiences: Bennett, Bakker & Field (2018), J. Organizational Behavior (54 samples, 299 effect sizes): psychological detachment had the strongest negative relationship with fatigue; control experiences had the strongest positive relationship with vigor. Correlational. [V] — [DOI 10.1002/job.2217](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2217)

### Inferences
- Triage question for the app: "Sleepy (could fall asleep) or mentally drained (wired but can't focus)?" Sleepy → 10–20 min nap with alarm, optionally coffee immediately before; drained → 5–10 min microbreak away from screen, brisk walk, daylight or window view of greenery.
- Nap cap at about 20 minutes and before mid-afternoon to limit sleep inertia and night-sleep disruption (inference from Brooks & Lack and Dutheil).
- "Drink a glass of water" is a harmless low-effort suggestion but should be labelled preliminary.

### Gaps
- No verified meta-analysis that upbeat music raises subjective energy in fatigued non-exercising adults; treat as preliminary.
- No verified trial showing water intake improves fatigue in already-hydrated adults (Pross et al. 2014 not retrieved).
- Microbreak meta-analysis could not say which break content is best; types were heterogeneous.
- Caffeine-nap evidence is from very small driving-simulator studies (n about 12) in sleep-restricted people.

## 4. OVERWHELM: planning, offloading, breaking tasks down, brief recovery

### Takeaway
The most direct experimental evidence is that writing a specific plan for unfinished goals removes their intrusive mental load (you do not have to complete the task), and that a 5-minute specific to-do list at bedtime speeds sleep onset. If-then planning has a medium-to-large meta-analytic effect on goal attainment, and recovery experiences (detachment, relaxation, mastery, control) are robustly linked to lower fatigue and better well-being, though mostly correlationally.

### Cited Findings
- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), JPSP 101(4), 667–683: unfulfilled goals intrude on thought and interfere with other tasks (Zeigarnik-type effects); letting participants make a specific plan for the goal eliminated these activation and interference effects, without completing the goal. Student lab studies. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/a0024192](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/)
- Scullin et al. (2018), JEP: General 147(1), 139–146: 57 healthy adults aged 18–30; 5 minutes writing a to-do list for the next few days (vs listing completed tasks) before overnight polysomnography led to faster sleep onset; the more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep. No no-writing control. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000374](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374); [Baylor release](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/can-writing-your-dos-help-you-doze-baylor-study-suggests-jotting-down-tasks-can)
- Size of the Scullin effect ("about 9 minutes faster") is press-reported recollection: **[U] unverified**.
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38: meta-analysis of 94 tests; implementation intentions ("if situation X, then I will do Y") had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = .65. Outcome is goal attainment, not felt overwhelm. [U] — [DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- Bennett, Bakker & Field (2018): four recovery experiences (detachment, relaxation, mastery, control); detachment most strongly (negatively) related to fatigue; control most strongly (positively) related to vigor; challenge demands reduce detachment/relaxation/control more than hindrance demands. [V] — [DOI 10.1002/job.2217](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2217)
- Steed, Swider, Keem & Liu (2021), Journal of Management, "Leaving Work at Work": 198 samples; recovery positively related to resources, well-being and performance and negatively to strain; different conceptualisations of recovery (activities, experiences, states) only modestly inter-related. [V] — [DOI 10.1177/0149206319864153](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206319864153)
- Recovery experience framework origin: Sonnentag & Fritz (2007), J. Occupational Health Psychology 12(3). [U] — [DOI 10.1037/1076-8998.12.3.204](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8998.12.3.204)
- Albulescu et al. (2022): micro-breaks improve vigor/fatigue but recovery from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 minutes. [V] — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0272460](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- Tidying/decluttering: only observational evidence found. Saxbe & Repetti (2010), PSPB: in 60 dual-income couples, wives who described their homes as cluttered/unfinished had flatter diurnal cortisol slopes and increasing depressed mood across the day. No intervention tested. [U] — [DOI 10.1177/0146167209352864](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209352864)

### Inferences
- A two-step "overwhelm" activity is well grounded: (1) 5-minute brain-dump of everything pending, written specifically; (2) pick one item and write a when/where/first-step plan (if-then). The claim "this frees up mental space" is supported; "this reduces felt stress" is an extrapolation.
- "Break the task down" has no direct trial located here; it is supported indirectly via plan specificity (Masicampo; Scullin).
- After planning, offer a detachment activity (something absorbing and unrelated) or a control activity (user chooses) based on Bennett et al.
- Tidying a small area for 10 minutes could be offered as a "control/mastery" experience, flagged preliminary.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis found of brief interventions targeting the subjective state "overwhelm" in non-clinical adults; the construct is rarely measured directly.
- No experimental evidence found that decluttering reduces stress.
- Recovery meta-analyses are largely cross-sectional/diary; causal inferences are limited.

## 5. POSITIVE states (joy, calm, contentment, excitement): sustaining and channeling

### Takeaway
Positive affect is amplified by savoring strategies (being present, telling others, positive mental time travel, behavioural display) and dampened by fault-finding, distraction, suppression and negative mental time travel; sharing good news with a responsive person adds affect beyond the event itself. Positive activated moods modestly boost creativity and prosocial behaviour is reliably (if modestly) linked to well-being, so joy/excitement can be channeled into creative, social and kind acts.

### Cited Findings
- Quoidbach, Berry, Hansenne & Mikolajczak (2010), Personality and Individual Differences 49(5), 368–373: N = 282; compared four savoring and four dampening strategies on positive affect and life satisfaction; strategies affected different components; using a wider variety of savoring strategies ("regulatory diversity") predicted greater overall happiness. Cross-sectional self-report. [V] — [DOI 10.1016/j.paid.2010.03.048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.03.048)
- Strategy-level detail as I recall it (being present and positive mental time travel → positive affect; capitalizing → life satisfaction; distraction lowers positive affect; fault-finding and negative mental time travel lower life satisfaction): **[U] unverified**.
- Gable, Reis, Impett & Asher (2004), JPSP 87(2), 228–245: telling others about personal positive events ("capitalization") was associated with higher daily positive affect and well-being beyond the event itself; benefits larger when the listener responds actively and enthusiastically. [V] — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228); [author PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/GableReisImpettAsher_2004.pdf)
- Lambert et al. (2013), JSPR 30(1): experiments showing sharing positive experiences boosts positive affect. [V, secondary] — [DOI 10.1177/0265407512449400](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407512449400)
- Bryant, Smart & King (2005), J. Happiness Studies 6: students assigned to reminisce about positive memories (10 min, twice daily, one week) using cognitive imagery or memorabilia reported greater increases in percentage of time feeling happy than controls; imagery was at least as effective as memorabilia. [U] — [DOI 10.1007/s10902-005-3889-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-005-3889-4)
- Jose, Lim & Bryant (2012), J. Positive Psychology: 30-day diary; momentary savoring mediated and moderated the link between positive events and happy mood. [U] — [DOI 10.1080/17439760.2012.671345](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2012.671345)
- Fredrickson (2001), American Psychologist 56(3): broaden-and-build theory; positive emotions broaden thought-action repertoires and build lasting resources. Theory paper. [U] — [DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.56.3.218](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.56.3.218)
- Fredrickson & Branigan (2005), Cognition and Emotion 19(3): film-induced joy/contentment broadened attention scope and thought-action repertoires vs neutral. Small lab studies; the attentional-broadening effect has had mixed replications. [U] — [DOI 10.1080/02699930441000238](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699930441000238)
- Baas, De Dreu & Nijstad (2008), Psychological Bulletin 134(6): mood-creativity meta-analysis; positive moods produce more creativity than neutral moods (r about .15), driven by activating positive moods (happy, elated) rather than deactivating ones (calm, relaxed). [U] — [DOI 10.1037/a0012815](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012815)
- Curry et al. (2018), J. ### Inferences
- Map by arousal: high-arousal positive (joy, excitement) → share the news with someone responsive, creative or learning task, kind act or gift, upbeat social activity. Low-arousal positive (calm, contentment) → savor in the present, photo reminiscence, awe walk, gratitude message. The creativity meta-analysis suggests creative prompts fit excited/happy more than calm states.
- Do not prompt analysis of "why" one feels good or comparisons (fault-finding) when the user is positive; prompt attention, expression and sharing.
- For kindness prompts, favour informal, spontaneous, person-directed acts (Hui et al.) over abstract or obligatory ones.
- Touch suggestions should be consent-framed and optional (hug a willing partner/friend/pet; weighted blanket or self-touch as weaker object-touch alternative).

### Gaps
- Broaden-and-build's "build" pathway has limited brief-intervention evidence, and the attentional broadening effect has mixed replications; no specific replication paper was verified here.
- No verified RCT of brief hugs on mood; touch meta-analysis is dominated by massage and clinical/neonatal contexts.
- No verified experimental evidence on cooking for others; shared-meal evidence is cross-sectional.
- Evidence on what sustains calm/contentment specifically (as opposed to positive affect in general) is thin.
- Comedy-watching evidence is mainly for cortisol, with heterogeneous mood outcomes.

## 6. Cross-cutting: effect sizes, doses, populations, cautions

### Takeaway
Typical verified effects are small (d or g about 0.2–0.45; r about .13), mostly from students or selected samples and often single sessions; the app should present suggestions as "likely to help a bit" and personalise. The main cautions are social-anxiety/introversion for social prompts, sleep inertia for naps, displacement and passive use for screens/games, and consent for touch.

### Cited Findings
- Verified pooled effects: kindness → actor well-being δ = 0.28 ([Curry 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.02.014)); prosociality–well-being r = .13 ([Hui 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000298)); micro-breaks vigor d = .36, fatigue d = .35 ([Albulescu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)); nap alertness 0.29 ([Dutheil 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph181910212)); psychological loneliness interventions g = 0.43 ([Hickin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102066)); loneliness RCTs SMD = −0.47 ([Lasgaard 2022 abstract](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9593938/)); laughter cortisol −31.9% ([Kramer & Leitao 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286260)); dehydration cognition ES = −0.21 ([Wittbrodt 2018](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001682)).
- Verified doses: micro-break ≤10 min; nap about 10 min (5 too short); awe walk 15 min weekly; animal petting 10 min; to-do list writing 5 min; green view 40 s; touch sessions about 20 min mean with no duration effect. (Sources as above.)
- Replication status: prosocial spending survives registered replication but with small, method-dependent effects ([Aknin 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000191)); talking-to-strangers effect replicated in London ([Schroeder 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001118), [U]); attention restoration evidence is weaker than commonly presented ([Ohly 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/10937404.2016.1196155)); loneliness-intervention certainty rated low to very low ([Lasgaard 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001578)).
- Sandstrom & Boothby (2021), Self and Identity: mini meta-analysis showing people's fears about talking to strangers (not being liked, not enjoying it) exceed reality. [U] — [DOI 10.1080/15298868.2020.1816568](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298868.2020.1816568)
- Games as recovery: Reinecke (2009), J. Media Psychology 21(3): survey evidence that people use games for recovery from stress and strain, especially when fatigued. Correlational. [U] — [DOI 10.1027/1864-1105.21.3.126](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105.21.3.126)

### Inferences
- Time-box games (10–20 min) and frame them as a deliberate break; evidence is for short sessions, and no evidence here supports long sessions.
- For every social suggestion, give a lower-exposure sibling (text vs call; kind act vs conversation; nostalgia vs outreach).
- Evidence-strength labels in the dataset should drive UI confidence copy.

### Gaps
- Head-to-head comparisons of these activities for a given emotion essentially do not exist; rankings are inferential.
- Most samples are WEIRD students; adult general-population generalisation is assumed.
- Displacement harms of gaming in adults were not verified with a specific source in this session.
