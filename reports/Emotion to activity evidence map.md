# Let self-report steer; evidence ranks activities

Peer-reviewed research supports a usable emotion-to-activity map, but it does not support reading emotion from a short handwriting or drawing sample. The app is credible if a one-tap self-report drives recommendations and the vision-LLM output is shown as a tentative guess the user confirms or corrects. On the activity side, a handful of links carry meta-analytic weight and PubMed-confirmed numbers: calming activities for anger (g = −0.63, while venting and running do nothing, g = −0.02), music for stress (d ≈ 0.55), relaxation and breathwork for anxiety and stress (d ≈ 0.5; g ≈ −0.3), behavioural activation for low mood (SMD −0.74 in depressed samples), micro-breaks for fatigue (d ≈ 0.35) and best-possible-self writing for positive affect (d = 0.51). Creative activities the team cares most about (drawing, colouring, clay) rest on small single-session student experiments whose numbers are mostly not indexed in PubMed and so remain unconfirmed; cooking, knitting, photography and painting alone have only observational or preliminary support. Boredom, loneliness and overwhelm have thin intervention evidence, and almost every rule for a *combination* of emotions is an extrapolation from four tested single-emotion principles rather than a tested finding. Preferences and hobbies are defensible for choosing among activities the evidence already allows (preference accommodation: d = 0.15–0.28, dropout odds ratio 1.79), not for overriding the "avoid" list. Typical effects are small and short-lived, so the honest product copy is "may help a bit right now".

## How to read the evidence and verification labels

Every number in this report carries one of three verification labels, taken from the "Verification log (PubMed pass)" in each notes file and the per-citation CSVs in `data/verification/`. **PM** means the figure was confirmed against the PubMed abstract or PMC full text. **PM-part** means the paper is confirmed but the specific number or detail is not in the accessible text. **UNC** means unconfirmed: the paper is not indexed in PubMed (common for *Art Therapy*, *Psychology of Aesthetics*, *Journal of Positive Psychology*, books and conference papers), so its numbers come from search-engine abstracts, secondary sources or memory. UNC figures are reported as "the cited claim", not as established values, and should be checked against the paper before they reach a slide or a UI string. No publisher full text was opened beyond PMC, so even PM means abstract-level in most cases.

Evidence strength (strong / moderate / preliminary) is the researchers' own judgment of applicability to a self-guided, 5–30 minute activity for a non-clinical adult, not a grade taken from the papers. A recurring caveat applies across the map: the large effect sizes come from multi-week programmes, often therapist-led and in clinical samples, while the app delivers one short solo session. Applying programme-level effects to a single session is an extrapolation, and the true momentary effect is probably smaller. Row-level data for every link (activity, emotion, effect, dose, population, caution, preference tags, verification status) belongs in the companion dataset: `data/evidence_links.csv` / `evidence_links.json`, `data/activities.json`, `data/emotions.json`, `data/recommendations.json`, `data/combination_rules.json`, `data/combination_matrix.csv`, `data/safety.json` and `data/sources.csv`, described in `data/schema.md`.

## A 30-second drawing cannot measure emotion, so frame it as an expressive check-in

### What the validity literature shows

Graphology is pseudoscience. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found graphologists no better than laypeople at predicting job performance from 1,223 scripts (UNC: not in PubMed; confirmed from the university record) ([Neter & Ben-Shakhar 1989](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-predictive-validity-of-graphological-inferences-a-meta-analyt/)), and two studies with about 100 participants each found "no evidence" for graphology as a personality measure (PM) ([Dazzi & Pedrabissi 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/PR0.105.F.1255-1268)). People keep believing such readings because of illusory correlation between handwriting descriptors and trait words (PM) ([King & Koehler 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11218342/)). That matters directly for an LLM pipeline: a fluent reading such as "jagged strokes suggest tension" will feel valid to users and judges whether or not it is, and graphology folklore is abundant in training text.

Pen-tablet kinematics show a real but small signal that does not transfer to this app. The EMOTHAW dataset (129 Italian students, seven standardised tasks on a digitising tablet with pressure, tilt and in-air tracking) classified higher versus lower DASS anxiety, depression and stress scorers with **60–71% accuracy** (confirmed against the arXiv abstract) ([Likforman-Sulem et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12245)). Later papers reporting 82–100% reuse the same 129 people with noise augmentation and no external validation (PM, full text) ([Nolazco-Flores et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8875261/)). Three mismatches block transfer: the labels are questionnaire symptom levels between people, not a feeling in this minute; the hardware is a pen with true pressure and in-air data, whereas the Pointer Events specification has unsupported hardware report a constant pressure of 0.5 ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/PointerEvent/pressure)); and the tasks are fixed copy tasks, not free drawing. The notes found **no study** validating a 5–30 second finger drawing on a phone against momentary affect.

Static images and vision LLMs fare worse. No peer-reviewed evidence was found that Gemini-, Claude- or GPT-class models validly infer a person's state from handwriting or a drawing; the existing preprints measure text similarity to human expert interpretations (about 0.75), not accuracy against the drawer's actual state ([arXiv:2512.21360](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.21360v1)). The experts' own method is weak: the authoritative review found validity evidence for human figure drawings "even more limited" than for the Rorschach (PM) ([Lilienfeld, Wood & Garb 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/1529-1006.002)), and the one large deep-learning study of House-Tree-Person drawings (N = 4,196) could not separate depressed from non-depressed drawers (PM) ([Lin et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103734)). The absence of LLM validation rests on one search pass, so a dedicated search could find more, but nothing currently licenses decimal emotion scores from a doodle.

The one defensible foothold is deliberate depiction. When people were asked to draw a *named* emotion abstractly, an algorithm identified the intended emotion from colour drawings by non-artists **50.81%** of the time and from line drawings about 23%, against 16.7% chance; human observers reached 43% and 32% (PM, full text; 81 drawers analysed) ([Damiano et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10080919/)). Colour–emotion associations are highly shared across 30 nations (r = .88, N = 4,598; PM) ([Jonauskaite et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620948810)). This shows that people encode an intended feeling in colour and line; it does not show that someone who used red *is* angry, the best case is still wrong about half the time, and stress, anxiety and depression were not among the six emotions tested. The parallel from a far better-studied channel is sobering: facial configurations are not reliable or specific enough to be read as diagnostic of emotional state (PM for the citation) ([Barrett et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930)).

### The framing that survives scrutiny

The scientifically honest design keeps the timed drawing as the engaging front door and moves the measurement elsewhere. The prompt becomes "draw how you feel right now" with a colour palette, since colour carried roughly twice the signal of line. The LLM is constrained to describe visible features (colour, density, line quality) and offer a hedged guess — "this looks dense and intense to me; does 'tense' fit, or is it something else?" — with graphological and projective-sign reasoning explicitly forbidden in the prompt. The user's confirmation or correction, captured through one-tap labels or a valence–arousal grid, becomes the operative input for recommendations and outranks the model every time. Validated brief self-report instruments exist for this role: the Self-Assessment Manikin (PM) ([Bradley & Lang 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7916%2894%2990063-9)) and the PHQ-4 as an optional periodic screener rather than a per-session measure (PM) ([Kroenke et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.psy.50.6.613)); the Affect Grid citation is UNC.

Three implementation points follow. The JSON the LLMs return can keep numeric fields for internal routing, but user-facing output should be coarse labels plus the observed features that prompted them, because decimals imply a measurement precision that does not exist. Agreement between Gemini and Claude measures reliability, not validity — two models can agree because they share folk-psychology priors — so disagreement is a reason to show no guess and simply ask, while agreement is never proof. Stroke timing (durations, pauses, speed variability) is the feature family with the most consistent support and is worth logging as an experimental signal alongside each self-report; the credible roadmap claim is a within-person validation study on held-out users, since the stronger passive-sensing results are longitudinal and within-person, such as keystroke dynamics tracking weekly depression ratings in nine patients (PM, full text) ([Zulueta et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.9775)). The pitch line from the notes summarises the position: drawing is how you express it, a one-tap check is how we measure it, and the AI is a mirror, not a diagnostician.

## Twelve emotion classes, anchored to valence, tension and energy

The notes recommend a hybrid taxonomy: a small fixed set of discrete labels that the LLM emits and users recognise, each pinned to dimensional coordinates that the matching rules operate on. The labels are chosen to line up with validated self-report constructs so each has a defensible meaning: DASS separates stress, anxiety and depression as distinct but correlated scales (PM for the three-scale structure; inter-scale correlations PM-part) ([Lovibond & Lovibond 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967%2894%2900075-U)), which justifies keeping "stressed" and "anxious" apart; the POMS manual supplies fatigue and vigor as mood states separate from sadness and happiness (UNC, test manual). Ekman's six basic emotions omit exactly the states a wellbeing app needs, and the finding that 27 graded categories organise emotion reports better than valence and arousal alone (PM) ([Cowen & Keltner 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1702247114)) supports emitting several graded scores rather than one hard label.

The coordinates are computed from the Warriner word norms, the one dataset the researchers opened directly (13,915 English lemmas; PM) ([Warriner, Kuperman & Brysbaert 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-012-0314-x)), rescaled by a centring formula that is a design choice. They are ratings of words, not of felt states, and the arousal scale is compressed, which is why "restless" needs a theory-based adjustment. A single arousal axis also cannot represent "stressed and tired", which averages to the middle. Thayer's two-dimensional model solves this, and the 1994 paper's abstract states that its results "support a 2-dimensional biopsychological model of mood" (PM) ([Thayer, Newman & McClain 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.5.910)). The notes therefore derive two scores for the rule engine: `tension = max(stressed, anxious, angry, restless)` and `energy = energetic − max(tired, 0.5·bored)`. Full definitions belong in `data/emotions.json`.

Three limits apply. No peer-reviewed study validates this exact 12-class set or its scoring keys; the label-to-construct mapping is by content, and the thresholds the notes propose (active ≥ 0.4, high ≥ 0.75) are engineering defaults with no calibration to felt intensity. Second, "overwhelm" is in the team's brief but not in the proposed classes. It is rarely measured as a construct in any of the literatures reviewed, with stress and tension as the nearest proxy. The practical reading, which is this report's synthesis rather than a cited finding, is to offer "overwhelmed" as a user-facing label that routes to the stressed rule plus the planning activities described below. Third, mixed states are the expected case, not an edge case: a meta-analysis of 63 studies found mixed emotions reliably inducible (d = 0.77; PM) ([Berrios, Totterdell & Kellett 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00428)), and experience sampling of more than 11,000 people found positive and negative emotions "relatively frequently" co-occurring (PM) ([Trampe, Quoidbach & Taquet 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0145450)). The recommender should reason over the top two or three confirmed labels, never the argmax. No source was confirmed for how often specific pairs such as anger with sadness, boredom with loneliness, or fatigue with low mood co-occur; treat those pairings as plausible, not cited.

### Creative activities: distract or depict something positive, never vent

Creative activities divide into three evidence tiers. The top tier has meta-analyses with confirmed numbers: music listening for stress, and best-possible-self writing for positive affect, where shorter practices gave marginally larger effects (PM; the notes originally had this moderator reversed and corrected it) ([Carrillo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386)). The middle tier is the visual-art experiments that matter most for this app: single sessions of drawing, colouring or clay after a mood induction, mostly in undergraduates. Their consistent and practically useful finding is about *instruction*, not medium. Drawing something happy produced the largest mood gain, while drawing to vent was no better than a non-art control task ([Dalebroux, Goldstein & Winner 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-008-9105-1)); the same distract-beats-express pattern held for both sadness and anger ([Genuth & Drake 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000265)). Those papers are not indexed in PubMed, so sample sizes, minutes and results are UNC, and none reports a standardised effect size in its abstract. The colouring finding has one PubMed-confirmed anchor: 20 minutes of mandala colouring lowered anxiety relative to reading in 120 adults aged 55–75 ([Koo, Chen & Yeh 2020](https://doi.org/10.1155/2020/6964737)). Structure matters, since unguided mandala colouring did no better than free drawing in one experiment of 88 students (PM, full text) ([Mantzios & Giannou 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00056)).

The bottom tier is everything else the team listed. Painting and collage appear only as options inside mixed-media sessions; 45 minutes of free art-making lowered cortisol in 39 adults, but with no control group and an art therapist facilitating (PM, full text) ([Kaimal, Ray & Muniz 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/07421656.2016.1166832)). Knitting evidence is a cross-sectional survey of more than 3,500 self-selected knitters (UNC) ([Riley, Corkhill & Morris 2013](https://doi.org/10.4276/030802213X13603244419077)). Cooking evidence is a review of 11 group interventions, often in clinical or rehabilitation settings, that the authors call preliminary (PM, full text) ([Farmer, Touchton-Leonard & Ross 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1090198117736352)); no study isolates solo home cooking or baking. A daily diary of 658 students linked everyday creativity to higher positive affect the *next* day (UNC) ([Conner, DeYoung & Silvia 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049)). Reading fiction has no immediate wellbeing effect from a single exposure (PM) ([Carney & Robertson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0266323)). Therapist-led art therapy (SMD 0.38 on change scores, with improvement in only 18% of 217 outcomes; PM) ([Joschko et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.28709)) is an upper bound from a different delivery mode and should not be transferred to a self-guided prompt.

Writing needs separate handling because its time course is the opposite of drawing. Expressive writing about difficult experiences has a tiny average benefit (r = .075 across 146 studies; PM) ([Frattaroli 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823)) that emerges only at delayed follow-up (g = −0.12; PM) ([Guo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjc.12408)), and writing increases distress immediately afterwards (PM via the Smyth 1998 abstract; the fuller description in [Baikie & Wilhelm 2005](https://doi.org/10.1192/apt.11.5.338) is UNC). The app can honestly describe drawing, colouring, clay, music and dancing as "can help right now", expressive writing and journaling as "pays off over days or weeks and may feel heavier at first", and everyday creativity as "tends to lift tomorrow's mood". No dose-response trial compares 5, 10, 20 and 30 minutes for any visual-art activity; the shortest verified dose is about 20 minutes, so a 5-minute version is an extrapolation.

### Movement and nature: energy and tension now, mood with habit

The acute literature is the directly relevant evidence for "right now" suggestions, and its doses fit the app's window. Ten minutes of brisk walking raised energy and lowered tension (PM) ([Thayer 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.1.119)); ten minutes or more in nature lowered cortisol, most efficiently at 20–30 minutes (PM) ([Hunter, Gillespie & Chen 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00722)); a single bout of aerobic exercise produced a small drop in state anxiety (g = 0.16; PM) ([Ensari et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22370)). The widely cited half-standard-deviation boost in positive activated affect after one aerobic bout comes from a meta-analysis not indexed in PubMed and is UNC. The large programme-level effects — median effect −0.43 for depression and −0.42 for anxiety across 97 reviews (PM) ([Singh et al. 2023](https://www.pubmed.gov/36796860/)), resistance training, yoga, tai chi and dance — support the *habit*, not the single session, and most exercise-for-depression evidence is rated low certainty by its own authors.

Two matching rules come out of this literature with real support. Intensity must fit the emotion: running does not reduce anger, and exercise above the ventilatory threshold feels uniformly unpleasant, while self-selected intensity "appears to foster greater tolerance" (PM; the abstract does not say affect is more positive at self-selected pace) ([Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello 2011](https://doi.org/10.2165/11590680-000000000-00000)). Prompts should therefore say "at a pace that feels good". And "fatigue" needs splitting: exercise reliably raises energy acutely but, by the UNC Loy meta-analysis, does not reduce fatigue on average unless the bout is low-to-moderate and longer than 20 minutes. The rumination benefit of nature walks was tested only at 90 minutes in 38 adults (PM, full text) ([Bratman et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1510459112)), so a 10–30 minute version is an extrapolation. No acute experiment of any physical or nature activity on boredom was found, loneliness evidence is observational (28% lower odds of momentary loneliness with nature contact; PM, full text) ([Hammoud et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-03398-2)), and stretching has no acute mood evidence.

### Mind-body and cognitive techniques: strong for arousal, weak when brief

The strongest tier is arousal-lowering practice for anger, anxiety and stress, plus cognitive change for momentary negative emotion: across 306 experimental comparisons, reappraisal reduced negative emotion (d+ = 0.36), perspective-taking did better (d+ = 0.45), distraction helped modestly (d+ = 0.27) and concentrating on the feeling made things worse (d+ = −0.26) (all PM) ([Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600)). Single sessions of 5–20 minutes produce measurable immediate changes for breathing, muscle relaxation, imagery, reappraisal and distraction; symptom-level change has been shown mainly with weeks of practice. Mindfulness illustrates the gap. Eight-week programmes reduce anxiety (SMD 0.38) and depression (0.30) in clinical populations (PM) ([Goyal et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018)), but brief mindfulness in novices has a small effect on negative affect (g = 0.21) that falls to **0.04 after bias correction** (PM) ([Schumer, Lindsay & Creswell 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000324)). A five-minute meditation should not be sold as evidence-based stress relief on the strength of the programme literature.

Several popular techniques have little or no direct support. Grounding (5-4-3-2-1) has no peer-reviewed trial in the notes and is justified only indirectly through distraction; label it practice-based. Box breathing appears as a single trial arm; counting to ten for anger has no standalone trial; hydration helps only by correcting dehydration; body scan has no component-specific estimate. Self-compassion exercises show sizeable cited effects (stress g = 0.67, depressive symptoms 0.66) but the meta-analysis is not in PubMed, so these are UNC, and group formats outperformed self-guided ones. Gratitude raises wellbeing only against doing nothing (d = 0.31) and shows no advantage over other activities (d = −0.03; PM) ([Davis et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/cou0000107)), which is a useful general warning: effects against active controls are consistently smaller than against waitlists, so much of the benefit of any of these activities is plausibly non-specific — doing something structured and pleasant.

### Social and playful activities: the barrier is misprediction

For loneliness the meta-analytic message is that changing maladaptive social cognition beats simply adding contact, and the single-session experiments explain why: people underestimate how much others will appreciate being contacted and overestimate awkwardness. That makes a one-line reframe ("they will appreciate it more than you expect") an evidence-aligned part of any reach-out prompt. These experiments measure felt connection and mood after one interaction, not chronic loneliness, and certainty across loneliness trials is rated low to very low. Affectionate touch has a large evidence base (state anxiety g = 0.64, depression g = 0.59 in adults across 137 studies; PM, full text) ([Packheiser et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01841-8)), but it is dominated by massage-type interventions and requires a willing partner. Watching comedy lowered cortisol by about 32% across eight studies (PM, full text) ([Kramer & Leitao 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286260)), with heterogeneous mood outcomes. Casual video games reduced stress and anxiety across 28 studies with no pooled estimate (PM) ([Pallavicini, Pepe & Mantovani 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/28150)); board games have little direct mood or boredom evidence. Head-to-head comparisons of these activities for a given emotion essentially do not exist, so any within-emotion ranking is inferential.

## Combination rules compose four tested principles; the pairs themselves are untested

No meta-analysis compares activity classes by emotion combination, and the notes found no direct trial for any negative pair as a pair. What exists are four principles, each tested for single emotions, that the rule engine composes.

The first is **arousal direction**: lower arousal for high-arousal negative states and activate for low-arousal ones. This is directly and strongly tested for anger only ([Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414), PM); for anxiety, stress, sadness, fatigue and boredom it is an inference from separate literatures. The second is **intensity gating**: across three experiments people chose distraction for high-intensity negative stimuli and reappraisal for low-intensity ones (PM) ([Sheppes et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611418350)). That is evidence about choice with pictures and electric shocks; the claim that reappraisal is *less effective* at high intensity is PM-part, and applying the gate to everyday stress is an extrapolation. The third is **controllability**: in 170 adults, higher reappraisal ability went with lower depression when stress was uncontrollable and *higher* depression when it was controllable (PM; an erratum exists and was not opened) ([Troy, Shallcross & Mauss 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613496434)). It implies one question when stress is high — "is this something you can act on right now?" — routing yes to planning and no to reappraisal or soothing. The fourth is **energy cost**: low-effort interventions were liked most and high-effort ones reduced stress most in a four-week study of 86 people (UNC, conference paper) ([Howe et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3502027)).

The tie-break when needs conflict, as in tense and tired, is to satisfy the tension need first with a low-energy-cost activity. That ordering is itself an extrapolation. The proposed procedure is a safety gate, then selection of the top two or three confirmed emotions, then the intensity gate, then the pair rule (falling back to the dominant emotion's rule), then personalisation within the allowed classes, then a "did that help?" prompt. The table gives the main pairs; the full rule set with rule identifiers, activity-class vocabulary and the pairwise matrix belongs in `data/combination_rules.json` and `data/combination_matrix.csv`.

## Preferences choose within the allowed set, never against the "avoid" list

The evidence for using preferences is real but modest and comes mostly from therapy, not from hobby-matched micro-activities. Clients given their preferred treatment showed better satisfaction (d = .34), completion and clinical outcome (d = .15) across 32 trials (PM) ([Lindhiem et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2014.06.002)); accommodating preferences across 53 psychotherapy studies cut dropout (OR = 1.79) and improved outcomes (d = 0.28) (PM) ([Swift et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30091140/)). For positive activities specifically, self-selection into the intervention moderated effects in a meta-analysis of 51 interventions (PM) ([Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.20593)), but the one study that matched exercises to stated preference found higher liking and only a non-significant wellbeing gain with no adherence difference (UNC) ([Schueller 2011](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/to-each-his-own-well-being-boosting-intervention-using-preference-to-guide-selection-13175/)). No trial shows that matching activities to *hobbies* improves momentary mood. The defensible claim is that preference matching improves uptake and satisfaction and gives a small outcome benefit.

That supports a two-stage design. The emotion rule defines the allowed activity classes and the avoid list; preferences only rank options inside the allowed set. The notes propose a transparent scoring formula as a design proposal, not a validated algorithm: evidence weight for the class given the emotion pattern, plus a bonus for matching a liked hobby, plus past "that helped" feedback, minus a penalty for recent repeats, with dislikes as a hard filter. A liked activity never unlocks an avoided one: a runner who is angry still gets calming options first, and a user who loves journaling and is acutely distressed still does not get "write about what's upsetting you" as the first suggestion. Activity-level preference tags (creative, musical, physical, social, solo, outdoor, hands-on, low-effort and so on) are carried in `data/activities.json` for this purpose.

Four further findings shape the picker. Showing two or three options and letting the user choose is supported on its own terms: autonomous motivation is consistently associated with exercise adherence across 66 mostly non-experimental studies (PM) ([Teixeira et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)), and free-choice colouring reduced anxiety more than forced-choice colouring in the cited claim (UNC) ([Eaton & Tieber 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/07421656.2016.1277113)). Skill gates matter for some activities and not others: cortisol reduction from art-making did not differ by prior art experience in an underpowered sample of 39 (PM, full text), so drawing, colouring, clay and music listening are safe defaults for anyone, whereas the instrument and knitting evidence comes entirely from people who already had the skill, so those should appear only when the user's profile lists them. The *reason* for an activity matters: in daily life music reduced stress most when the listener's stated goal was relaxation (PM) ([Linnemann et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26142566/)), so the prompt should carry the intent ("to unwind") rather than just the activity. And when the user is tired, default to the low-effort option while keeping one higher-effort option visible. Rotating suggestions for variety is reasonable, but the supporting study concerned adaptation after positive life changes, so applying it to activity rotation is an extrapolation (PM for the study) ([Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212436400)). For a hackathon a rule-based ranker with a feedback counter is sufficient; a contextual-bandit recommender needs weeks of per-user data, and the published example showed only a tendency toward better coping (UNC).

## Guardrails: no diagnosis, corrected labels, a crisis route, and activity-level cautions

**Claims and wording.** Mental-health apps deliver small average benefits — depression g = 0.28 and generalised anxiety g = 0.26 across 176 randomised trials, larger against inactive controls (PM) ([Linardon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.21183)) — and harms are poorly reported: only 55 of 171 trials reported adverse events, with a pooled deterioration rate of 6.7% that did not differ from controls (PM) ([Linardon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-024-01388-y)). The app should say "may help", use state words ("low", "worried") rather than clinical terms, phrase inferences as "it looks like you might be feeling…", always offer "that's not right — let me pick", and carry a plain statement that it is not a medical device and does not diagnose or treat. Staying inside general-wellness claims is also the regulatory posture described in secondary summaries of the FDA's updated January 2026 guidance, which the researchers did not open directly ([FDA general wellness guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download)).

**Crisis route.** A review of 69 depression and suicide-prevention apps found that only 7% included all six recommended suicide-prevention strategies and 9% listed a wrong crisis helpline number (PM) ([Martinengo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1461-z)). Crisis resources must therefore be localised and checked by a human, not generated. The notes propose skipping the activity list and showing support information when negative states are extreme, persist across sessions, or free text mentions self-harm or hopelessness. No validated threshold exists for app-detected escalation, so this is a precaution, not a screening tool. Given the validity findings above, this report's synthesis is that the gate should key on the user's own self-report and words rather than on a drawing-derived score, which can neither rule distress in nor out. A signpost to professional help for low mood lasting more than two weeks belongs in the standard disclaimer.

**Activity-level cautions.** The evidence identifies specific backfires that the rule engine should encode, with the full list in `data/safety.json`:

- **Venting** in any medium for anger or acute sadness: ineffective or worse (PM for the anger meta-analysis; the drawing studies are UNC).
- **Expressive or trauma writing** as an immediate fix: mood dips right afterwards; never offer it to a highly distressed user with no follow-up.
- **Sad music** for users prone to rumination: depression scores rose in high ruminators in the cited claim (UNC) ([Garrido & Schubert 2015](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2015.32.4.313)).
- **Meditation**: adverse events had a pooled prevalence of 8.3% across 83 studies (PM) ([Farias et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)), and in 8-week programmes 58% reported at least one negative-valence effect and about 6% lasting bad effects (PM, full text) ([Britton et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702621996340)). Keep practices short, offer an exit and an alternative.
- **Breathwork**: slow, long-exhale styles only; exclude hyperventilation and long breath-holds. Medical cautions for breath-holding were not sourced and are standard practice guidance.
- **Exercise**: default to light or moderate self-paced activity; show vigorous options only to regular exercisers without cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, following the ACSM screening logic (PM for the citation) ([Riebe et al. 2015](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180)); suppress vigorous options in the hour before bed (PM; the finding rests on one study) ([Stutz, Eiholzer & Spengler 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0)).
- **Naps**: about 10 minutes with an alarm, early in the day. **Cold immersion**: no immediate mood or stress benefit in randomised trials; the cardiac caution is general guidance without a sourced reference.
- **Social prompts**: always pair with a lower-exposure sibling (text before call, kind act before conversation). **Touch**: consent-framed and optional. **Spending on others**: not for users in financial stress. **Performance**: singing lowered cortisol in rehearsal and raised it before an audience (PM, full text) ([Fancourt, Aufegger & Williamon 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01242)), so frame singing, playing and dancing as private and non-evaluative.
- **Never suggest** alcohol, comfort eating or passive scrolling; time-box games to 10–20 minutes.

**Data and regulation.** Drawings, stroke dynamics and emotion labels are sensitive in practice; sending them to third-party LLM APIs needs explicit consent and minimisation, and GDPR analysis was not researched. The EU AI Act prohibits AI systems that infer emotions in workplace and education settings (wording read on an unofficial consolidated site) ([Article 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/)) and requires deployers of emotion-recognition systems to inform the people exposed ([Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). Whether stroke dynamics count as biometric data under the Act's definition is unsettled, and application dates come from secondary legal sources that disagree with each other. The safe posture, offered as an inference and not legal advice, is to disclose the inference up front, never sell into employers or schools, and keep the user's self-report as the operative input, which both weakens the "AI infers your emotion" characterisation and matches the science.

## Conclusion

The evidence reverses the app's apparent centre of gravity. The novel part, reading emotion from a timed drawing, is the part with no validation, while the unglamorous part, a rule table mapping self-reported states to activities, is where the defensible numbers live. A credible build therefore treats the drawing as expression and engagement, the tap as measurement, and the model as a describer whose guesses are logged against corrections. That log is also the product's scientific asset: paired drawings, stroke timings and same-session self-reports are exactly the dataset the field lacks for phone-based, momentary, within-person inference.

On the activity side, the map is strongest where it tells the app what *not* to do: no venting for anger, no "write about it" for acute distress, no sad-music loops for ruminators, no sitting quietly for boredom, no hard exercise sold as a fatigue cure. These are better supported than most positive recommendations and cheap to implement. The largest remaining uncertainties are structural rather than fixable by more reading: single-session doses of 5–10 minutes are below what most studies tested, combination rules are compositions rather than findings, and nearly half of the creative-arts citations (23 of 47) could not be fully confirmed in PubMed. Shipping evidence-strength and verification labels with every suggestion, and letting "did that help?" feedback re-rank within the allowed set, turns those gaps into something the app measures instead of something it hides.
